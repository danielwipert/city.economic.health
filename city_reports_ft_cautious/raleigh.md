# Raleigh-Cary

**Grade: A (Very Good) | 70.7th percentile | October 2026**

---

The Raleigh-Cary metro area has earned an overall grade of A, ranking in the 70.7th percentile among 50 US metros, with a strong labor demand and low unemployment rate defining its current economic character, at 98th and 94th percentiles, respectively. The labor demand composite score of 8.63, driven by a 2.23% employment growth rate and a 1.207% weekly hours deviation above trend, signals a genuine demand expansion. This combination of metrics suggests a thriving job market.

**Labor Demand**
The Raleigh-Cary metro area has seen a 2.23% employment growth rate and a 1.207% weekly hours deviation above its own trend, indicating a strong labor demand. This combination signals a genuine demand expansion, where jobs are being added and hours are running above trend. The labor demand composite score of 8.63 further reinforces this notion, placing the city in the top tier of labor demand.

**Unemployment**
The unemployment rate in Raleigh-Cary stands at 3.10%, which is in the top tier, indicating a tight labor market. This means that businesses may face challenges in hiring, as the pool of available workers is relatively small, and may need to offer competitive wages to attract talent. The low unemployment rate also suggests that workers have more bargaining power.

**Wage Growth**
The year-over-year wage growth in Raleigh-Cary is 3.16%, which is below average, ranking in the 38th percentile. This moderate wage growth rate implies that labor costs for employers are rising, but at a slower pace, and worker purchasing power is increasing, but not rapidly. This may be a concern for businesses looking to manage labor costs.

**Cost of Living**
Raleigh-Cary has a cost of living score that places it in the 82nd percentile, with a PSF of $213/sqft and an average hourly earnings of $38.60/hr, resulting in a ratio of 5.52. The fact that PSF is falling by 3.2% YoY relative to wages makes the city more affordable. This affordability advantage can be a significant talent attraction factor, as workers may not require wage premiums to maintain their standard of living.

The civilian labor force in Raleigh-Cary has contracted by 1.63% year-over-year, indicating a shrinking labor pool. This decline in labor force growth poses a structural headwind for hiring, as businesses may struggle to find available workers to fill open positions. The implications for hiring capacity are concerning, as the city's labor market may become increasingly competitive.

**Building Permits**
The number of residential building permits in Raleigh-Cary has decreased by 5.68% year-over-year, signaling a tightening of housing supply. This decline in building permits may lead to a supply squeeze, potentially eroding future affordability and making it more challenging for workers to relocate to the city. The decrease in permits is a concern for businesses relying on a steady influx of new talent.

**Days on Market**
The median days on market for homes in Raleigh-Cary is 59 days, with no year-over-year change. This relatively fast pace of home sales indicates a competitive market, which may make it challenging for relocating workers to find affordable housing. However, the stable days on market suggests that the market is not overheating, and workers may still find opportunities to purchase or rent homes.

**Office Economy**
Raleigh-Cary has a deep professional talent pool, with an office economy score ranking in the 98th percentile. This makes the city an attractive location for businesses in the tech, finance, consulting, and HQ sectors, which require specialized knowledge-economy talent. The city's strong office economy is a significant advantage for businesses looking to tap into a skilled workforce.

The Raleigh-Cary metro area offers businesses a unique combination of strong labor demand, low unemployment, and affordability, making it an attractive location for companies looking to expand or relocate. However, the single biggest risk or constraint for decision-makers is the shrinking labor force and tightening housing supply, which may lead to increased competition for talent and higher labor costs in the long run.
