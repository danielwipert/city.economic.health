# Richmond

**Grade: D (Emergency) | 25.1th percentile | November 2026**

---

The Richmond metro area has an overall grade of D, ranking in the 25.1th percentile out of 50 US metros, with a labor demand composite score of 2.96, placing it in the bottom tier. The city's economic character is most defined by its weak labor demand, with employment growth of -0.96% YoY, and its rapidly expanding housing supply, with building permits increasing by 130.84% YoY. These metrics suggest a city with significant challenges in job creation, but with a potential for improved affordability in the future.

**Labor Demand**
The employment growth rate in Richmond is -0.96% YoY, and weekly hours are deviating from the trend by -0.122%, signaling a contraction in labor demand. This combination indicates a lack of genuine demand expansion, with the city experiencing a decline in both jobs and working hours. As a result, businesses may face a challenging environment for growth and expansion.

**Unemployment**
The unemployment rate in Richmond is 3.80%, which is below average, ranking in the 34th percentile. This suggests a relatively tight labor market, with some slack, but still presenting challenges for businesses trying to hire, as they may face upward pressure on wages. However, the market is not extremely tight, and businesses may still be able to find suitable candidates.

**Wage Growth**
The year-over-year wage growth in Richmond is +1.57%, which is relatively slow, ranking in the 16th percentile. This moderate wage growth rate implies that labor costs for employers are rising, but at a manageable pace, while worker purchasing power is increasing, but not rapidly. As a result, businesses may need to balance their labor costs with the need to attract and retain talent.

**Cost of Living**
Richmond has a cost of living score that places it in the bottom tier, with a percentile rank of 18th, and a PSF to earnings ratio of $226/sqft vs $35.71/hr, resulting in a ratio of 6.33. This suggests that the city is relatively expensive, making it challenging for businesses to attract talent without offering wage premiums. The fact that PSF is increasing by 0.4% YoY further exacerbates the affordability issue.

**Labor Force Growth**
The civilian labor force in Richmond is contracting at a rate of -2.25% YoY, indicating a shrinking labor pool. This decline in labor force supply presents a structural headwind for hiring, as businesses may struggle to find qualified candidates to fill open positions. As a result, companies may need to invest in training and development programs to upskill the existing workforce.

**Building Permits**
The number of building permits in Richmond has increased by 130.84% YoY, signaling a rapid expansion of housing supply. This growth in permits suggests that the city's housing market is poised for improved affordability, which could, in turn, enhance its attractiveness to workers and businesses. However, it remains to be seen whether this increased supply will be sufficient to meet demand and drive down costs.

**Days on Market**
The median days on market in Richmond is currently 44 days, with a YoY decrease of -2.2%. This relatively fast pace of home sales suggests a competitive market, which may make it challenging for relocating workers to find suitable housing. As a result, businesses may need to offer relocation assistance or other incentives to attract talent to the area.

**Office Economy**
Richmond's professional and office worker share is ranked in the 22nd percentile, indicating a relatively shallow talent pool. This suggests that the city is less suited for businesses that require a deep knowledge-economy talent pool, such as tech, finance, or consulting firms. However, it may be more suitable for companies with industrial or logistics-oriented operations.

The Richmond metro area presents a challenging environment for businesses, with weak labor demand, a relatively expensive cost of living, and a shrinking labor force. However, the city's rapidly expanding housing supply and moderate wage growth rate offer some potential for future improvement. The single biggest risk or constraint for decision-makers is the city's limited talent pool, which may require businesses to invest in training and development programs or offer relocation incentives to attract qualified candidates.
