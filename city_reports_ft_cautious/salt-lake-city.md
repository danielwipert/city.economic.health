# Salt Lake City-Murray

**Grade: A (Very Good) | 66.0th percentile | July 2026**

The Salt Lake City-Murray metro area has earned an overall grade of A, ranking in the 66.0th percentile among 50 US metros, with a strong labor demand and a deep professional talent pool being the two metrics that most define its current economic character, driven by a 90th percentile labor demand composite score and a 96th percentile office/professional worker share. This combination of metrics suggests a city with a strong foundation for business growth.

**Labor Demand**
The Salt Lake City-Murray metro area has seen a 2.73% year-over-year employment growth rate, combined with a -0.912% weekly hours deviation from its own trend, signaling genuine demand expansion. This indicates that the city is adding jobs at a rapid pace, with hours worked being slightly below trend, suggesting a healthy labor market. The labor demand composite score of 7.21 places the city in the top tier, at the 90th percentile.

**Unemployment**
The unemployment rate in Salt Lake City-Murray stands at 3.40%, which is at the 72nd percentile, indicating a relatively tight labor market. This means that businesses may face challenges in hiring, as the pool of available workers is smaller, and may need to offer competitive wages to attract talent. The tight labor market also suggests that workers may have more bargaining power.

**Wage Growth**
The city has experienced a 4.19% year-over-year wage growth rate, placing it near the median at the 56th percentile. This moderate wage growth rate suggests that labor costs for employers are rising, but at a manageable pace, and worker purchasing power is increasing. While this is not exceptionally high, it still indicates a positive trend for workers and a potential cost consideration for businesses.

**Cost of Living**
With a cost of living ratio of $266/sqft to $38.98/hr, resulting in a ratio of 6.82, Salt Lake City-Murray ranks at the 57th percentile in terms of affordability. This near-median ranking indicates that the city is neither exceptionally affordable nor expensive compared to its peers. The relatively stable cost of living means that businesses may not need to offer significant wage premiums to attract talent, but it also does not provide a strong competitive advantage in terms of affordability.

**Labor Force Growth**
The civilian labor force in Salt Lake City-Murray has grown at a -0.06% year-over-year rate, placing it at the 64th percentile. This slight contraction in the labor force supply suggests that hiring capacity may be limited, and businesses may face challenges in finding available workers. However, the small negative growth rate indicates that this is not a significant constraint at present.

**Building Permits**

This sharp decline in building permits suggests that housing supply is tightening, which could lead to future affordability issues and challenges in accommodating a growing workforce. This trend may signal potential risks for businesses relying on a mobile workforce or looking to expand their operations.

**Days on Market**
The median days on market for homes in Salt Lake City-Murray stands at 50 days, with a 4.2% year-over-year increase. This indicates a relatively accessible market for workers relocating to the city, as homes are not selling exceptionally quickly. The rising days on market, despite a strong job market, may suggest a healthy normalization of the housing market.

**Office Economy**
With an office/professional worker share of 4.40, placing it at the 96th percentile, Salt Lake City-Murray has a deep and talented professional pool. This makes the city highly suited for businesses in the tech, finance, consulting, and HQ sectors, but less ideal for those in industrial or logistics-dominant economies. The strong office economy is a significant advantage for businesses looking to tap into a knowledgeable and skilled workforce.

The Salt Lake City-Murray metro area offers businesses a strong labor market, a deep professional talent pool, and a relatively stable cost of living. However, the single biggest risk or constraint for decision-makers is the tightening housing supply, signaled by the sharp decline in residential building permits, which could lead to future affordability issues and challenges in accommodating a growing workforce. This consideration should be factored into any business location decision to ensure long-term viability and success in the area.
