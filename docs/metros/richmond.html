Economic Analysis

The Richmond metro area has an overall grade of C- with a composite score ranking it 30.7th percentile out of 50 US metros. The city's economic character is most defined by its weak labor demand, with a labor demand composite score of 3.24, and its low office economy share, ranking 22nd percentile. Specifically, the employment growth rate of -0.76% and weekly hours deviation of -0.415% are key metrics that shape the city's current economic landscape.

Labor Demand

The employment growth rate in Richmond is -0.76%, and weekly hours are deviating -0.415% from the city's own 12-month baseline, signaling a contraction in labor demand. This combination indicates a lack of genuine demand expansion, with payrolls contracting and hours below trend. The scenario suggests that the city is experiencing a decline in job creation and working hours.

Unemployment

The unemployment rate in Richmond is 3.80%, ranking 56th percentile, which indicates a near-median level of unemployment. This rate suggests that the market has some slack, making it relatively easier for businesses to hire compared to tighter labor markets. However, the practical implication for a business trying to hire in Richmond is that it may still face some competition for talent, but not as intense as in cities with lower unemployment rates.

Wage Growth

The year-over-year wage growth in Richmond is +2.49%, ranking 32nd percentile, which is below average. This moderate wage growth rate implies that labor costs for employers are rising, but not as quickly as in other cities. For workers, this means that their purchasing power is increasing, but not at a rapid pace.

Cost of Living

Richmond has a cost of living score ranking 24th percentile, with a PSF of $229/sqft and an earnings ratio of $35.92/hr, resulting in a ratio of 6.38. This indicates that the city is relatively expensive compared to its peers. The implication for talent attraction is that businesses may need to offer wage premiums to compensate for the higher cost of living, making it less competitive in attracting talent without additional incentives.

Labor Force Growth

The civilian labor force in Richmond is growing at a rate of -0.80% year-over-year, indicating a contraction in the labor force supply. This decline suggests that the city's workforce is shrinking, which could pose a structural headwind for hiring and business expansion.

Building Permits

The number of building permits in Richmond has increased by +8.22% year-over-year, signaling an expansion in housing supply. This growth in permits suggests that developer confidence is rising, and future housing supply is likely to increase, which could improve affordability and accommodate a growing workforce.

Days on Market

The median days on market in Richmond is currently 40 days, with a year-over-year decrease of -7.0%. This rapid sales pace indicates a competitive market, making it challenging for relocating workers to find homes. The low days on market suggests that the city's housing market is hot, and businesses may need to consider this when relocating employees or planning for workforce expansion.

Office Economy

Richmond's office economy share ranks 22nd percentile, indicating a relatively shallow professional talent pool. This suggests that the city is less suited for businesses that require a deep knowledge-economy talent pool, such as tech, finance, or consulting firms, but may be more suitable for industries with fewer specialized roles, such as logistics or manufacturing.

The Richmond metro area offers a business a relatively affordable housing supply expansion, but its weak labor demand, low office economy share, and shrinking labor force pose significant risks. The single biggest constraint for a decision-maker is the city's limited talent pool and declining labor force, which could hinder business growth and expansion plans.
