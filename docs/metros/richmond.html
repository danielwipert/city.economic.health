Economic Analysis

The Richmond metro area has an overall grade of C- with a composite score ranking it 35.3th percentile out of 50 US metros. The city's economic character is most defined by its low labor demand, with a labor demand composite score of 2.93, ranking in the bottom tier at the 8th percentile, and its high building permits growth, with a year-over-year change of +83.77%, ranking in the top tier at the 96th percentile. These metrics signal a city with struggling job market fundamentals but strong developer confidence.

Labor Demand

The employment growth rate in Richmond is -0.82% year-over-year, and weekly hours are deviating from the trend by +0.219%. This combination signals a contraction in labor demand, as hours are slightly above trend during a period of job losses, indicating a survivor squeeze where remaining workers are absorbing the load of eliminated roles. This suggests that businesses may face challenges in finding skilled workers to fill new positions.

Unemployment

The unemployment rate in Richmond is 3.60%, which ranks in the above-average tier at the 66th percentile. This indicates a relatively tight labor market with some slack, making it moderately challenging for businesses to hire new workers. As a result, businesses may need to offer competitive wages to attract top talent in this market.

Wage Growth

The year-over-year wage growth in Richmond is +2.40%, which is below average, ranking in the 30th percentile. This moderate wage growth rate suggests that labor costs for employers are rising, but at a slower pace, and worker purchasing power is increasing, but not rapidly. This may impact businesses' ability to attract and retain workers, as they may need to balance wage growth with other benefits.

Cost of Living

Richmond has a cost of living score that ranks in the 29th percentile, with a price-to-salary ratio of $226/sqft to $35.92/hr, resulting in a ratio of 6.29. This indicates that the city is relatively expensive compared to its peers, making it less attractive for talent attraction without wage premiums. Businesses may need to consider offering higher salaries to compensate for the high cost of living.

Labor Force Growth

The civilian labor force in Richmond is shrinking at a rate of -2.57% year-over-year, indicating a contracting labor supply. This signals a structural headwind for hiring, as the pool of available workers is decreasing, making it more challenging for businesses to find skilled workers.

Building Permits

The year-over-year change in building permits in Richmond is +83.77%, which is in the top tier, ranking in the 96th percentile. This rapid growth in building permits suggests that housing supply is expanding, which may improve affordability and workforce accommodation in the future. This is a positive signal for businesses considering relocation, as it may lead to a more stable and affordable housing market for their workers.

Days on Market

The current median days on market in Richmond is 44 days, with a year-over-year decrease of -4.3%. This indicates a relatively fast-paced market, making it challenging for relocating workers to find housing. The decreasing days on market suggests that the market is becoming even more competitive, which may impact businesses' ability to attract and retain workers.

Office Economy

Richmond has an office economy score that ranks in the 22nd percentile, indicating a relatively shallow professional talent pool. This suggests that the city is less suited for businesses that require a deep knowledge-economy talent pool, such as tech or finance companies, but may be more suitable for industries with fewer specialized roles, such as logistics or manufacturing.

The Richmond metro area offers businesses a unique combination of struggling job market fundamentals and strong developer confidence, with a rapidly expanding housing supply. However, the single biggest risk or constraint for decision-makers is the city's low labor demand and contracting labor supply, which may make it challenging to find and retain skilled workers.
