Economic Analysis

Columbus, Ohio, has an overall grade of C+, scoring 47.7 out of 100 across 50 US metros, with its economy currently defined by slower job growth and a tight labor market, as evidenced by a mere 0.1% decline in employment over the past year. The city's unemployment rate of 3.3% is a notable strength, indicating a competitive job market where employers may face challenges in finding talent.

Labor Demand

Whether employers are adding jobs, based on job growth over the past year and changes in weekly work hours.

Columbus is losing jobs at a rate of 0.1% over the past year, and while workers' hours are rising by 0.5%, this modest increase is not enough to offset the weakness in job growth, suggesting a sluggish labor market. This means that employers may not be expanding their payrolls as quickly as in other metros, with a score of 18 out of 100, indicating weaker hiring than most cities.

Unemployment

The share of people who want a job but can't find one; lower is better.

The unemployment rate in Columbus is 3.3%, which is lower than most metros, earning a score of 84 out of 100, and making it a challenging environment for employers to find and hire new talent. With such a low unemployment rate, employers may need to offer competitive wages and benefits to attract workers, as the labor market is highly competitive.

Wage Growth

How much average hourly pay rose over the past year.

Wages in Columbus have risen by 4.5% over the past year, which is faster than in many other metros, with a score of 68 out of 100, indicating stronger pay growth. This means that employers may face rising labor costs, while workers can enjoy higher paychecks and increased purchasing power, making the city an attractive place to work.

Cost of Living

How many hours a local worker needs to work to pay for one square foot of a home, and whether that's improving.

The cost of living in Columbus is around average, with a price per square foot of $205 and hourly pay of $34.52, earning a score of 51 out of 100, and indicating that housing is neither particularly affordable nor unaffordable compared to other metros. However, with housing prices rising by 1.0% over the past year, the city's affordability may be decreasing, making it essential for employers to consider the impact on their employees' cost of living.

Labor Force Growth

Whether the number of people working or looking for work is growing.

The labor force in Columbus is shrinking at a rate of 2.0% over the past year, with a score of 12 out of 100, indicating slower workforce growth than most metros. This decline in the labor force means that employers may face challenges in finding new talent, as the pool of available workers is decreasing, making it essential to retain existing employees and attract new ones from other areas.

Building Permits

How many new homes builders got approval for, compared with a year ago.

New home approvals in Columbus have declined by 3.0% over the past year, earning a score of 50 out of 100, which is around average for the 50 metros. This decrease in building permits may signal a potential supply squeeze in the future, which could impact housing affordability and the ability of workers to relocate to the city, making it essential for developers and policymakers to address this issue.

Days on Market

How long a typical home for sale takes to find a buyer; about 35 to 80 days is healthiest.

Homes in Columbus are taking around 50 days to sell, with a 6.4% increase over the past year, earning a score of 72 out of 100, indicating a healthier housing market than most metros. This relatively stable housing market, with a moderate number of days on market, suggests that buyers have a reasonable amount of time to find a home, and sellers can expect to sell their properties without excessive delays, making the city an attractive place to live and work.

Office Economy

The share of jobs in office-based fields like tech, finance and professional services.

Columbus has a relatively small office-based workforce, with a score of 10 out of 100, indicating a smaller office workforce than most metros. This suggests that the city may be more suited to industries that are not heavily reliant on professional services, such as manufacturing or logistics, rather than tech, finance, or consulting, which may require a deeper talent pool.

The biggest strength of Columbus's economy is its low unemployment rate, which indicates a highly competitive labor market, while its biggest risk is the slower labor force growth, which may limit the city's ability to attract and retain talent, making it essential for employers and policymakers to address this issue to ensure the city's long-term economic growth and competitiveness. Overall, Columbus's economy is characterized by a mix of strengths and weaknesses, requiring careful consideration by businesses and individuals looking to locate or invest in the city.
