Economic Analysis

The Dallas-Fort Worth-Arlington metro area has an overall grade of B, ranking in the 52.6th percentile among 50 US metros, with a composite score driven largely by its above-average labor demand and cost of living. The city's labor demand composite score of 6.24, combining a 1.27% employment growth rate and a 0.162% weekly hours deviation above its own trend, suggests genuine demand expansion. Additionally, its cost of living, with a PSF of $202/sqft and an hourly wage of $37.13, resulting in a ratio of 5.44, ranks in the 69th percentile for affordability.

Labor Demand

The Dallas-Fort Worth-Arlington metro area exhibits a labor demand composite score of 6.24, driven by a 1.27% year-over-year employment growth rate and a 0.162% deviation in weekly hours above its own trend. This combination signals genuine demand expansion, indicating that the city is adding jobs and workers are putting in more hours, a sign of a healthy and growing economy. The above-average labor demand score of 78th percentile further reinforces this positive outlook.

Unemployment

The unemployment rate in Dallas-Fort Worth-Arlington stands at 4.00%, ranking in the 36th percentile, which indicates a below-average level of unemployment. This tight labor market implies that businesses may face challenges in hiring, as the pool of available workers is relatively small, and may need to offer competitive wages to attract talent. The low unemployment rate suggests that the city has a strong job market, but it also poses a challenge for companies looking to expand their workforce.

Wage Growth

The year-over-year wage growth in Dallas-Fort Worth-Arlington is 1.95%, ranking in the 26th percentile, indicating below-average wage growth. This moderate wage growth rate suggests that labor costs for employers are rising, but at a slower pace than in many other cities. While this may help keep costs under control for businesses, it also means that workers may not see significant increases in their purchasing power, potentially affecting consumer demand.

Cost of Living

Dallas-Fort Worth-Arlington has a cost of living score that ranks in the 69th percentile, with a PSF of $202/sqft and an hourly wage of $37.13, resulting in a ratio of 5.44. This indicates that the city is relatively affordable compared to its peers, making it an attractive location for talent without requiring significant wage premiums. The fact that PSF is decreasing by 2.4% year-over-year further enhances the city's affordability.

Labor Force Growth

The civilian labor force in Dallas-Fort Worth-Arlington is growing at a rate of 0.19% year-over-year, ranking in the 70th percentile. This positive growth rate indicates that the workforce supply is expanding, which is a favorable condition for businesses looking to hire. The growing labor force provides a larger pool of potential employees, reducing the risk of labor shortages and making it easier for companies to find the talent they need.

Building Permits

The number of building permits in Dallas-Fort Worth-Arlington has decreased by 22.31% year-over-year, ranking in the 14th percentile. This sharp decline in building permits suggests that the housing supply is tightening, which could lead to future affordability issues and challenges in accommodating a growing workforce. The decrease in permits may indicate a potential supply squeeze, making it essential for businesses to consider the long-term implications of this trend on their operations and employee relocation.

Days on Market

The median days on market for homes in Dallas-Fort Worth-Arlington is 51 days, with a year-over-year increase of 2.0%. This near-median ranking of 52nd percentile indicates a relatively balanced market. For workers relocating to this city, the current market conditions suggest a moderate level of competition, neither extremely competitive nor overly accessible.

Office Economy

The share of professional and office workers in Dallas-Fort Worth-Arlington is 4.06, ranking in the 90th percentile. This deep talent pool makes the city an attractive location for businesses in the tech, finance, consulting, and HQ sectors. The city's strong office economy is well-suited for knowledge-based industries, but it may be less suitable for companies primarily involved in industrial or logistics operations.

The Dallas-Fort Worth-Arlington metro area offers businesses a unique combination of above-average labor demand, relatively low unemployment, and an affordable cost of living. However, the single biggest risk or constraint for decision-makers to consider is the sharp decline in building permits, which may lead to future housing supply constraints and affordability issues, potentially affecting the city's attractiveness for relocating workers and the overall competitiveness of businesses operating in the area.
