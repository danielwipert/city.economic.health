Economic Analysis

The Portland-Vancouver-Hillsboro metro area has an overall grade of C+, scoring 49.3 out of 100 across 50 US metros, with its economy currently defined by a mix of slow job growth and rising wages. The city's employment growth has been sluggish, with a decline of 0.63% over the past year, while wages have risen by 5.71% over the same period.

Labor Demand

Whether employers are adding jobs, based on job growth over the past year and changes in weekly work hours.

The city is losing jobs, with a decline of 0.63% over the past year, and while workers' hours are rising by 0.5%, this is not enough to offset the job losses, indicating a weak job market. This means that employers may not be hiring as many new workers, and remaining workers are putting in extra hours to compensate for the reduced workforce.

Unemployment

The share of people who want a job but can't find one; lower is better.

The unemployment rate in Portland-Vancouver-Hillsboro is 4.10%, which is lower than many other metros, scoring 74 out of 100, making it relatively hard for employers to hire new workers. With a lower unemployment rate, employers may face more competition for talent and potentially higher wages to attract and retain workers.

Wage Growth

How much average hourly pay rose over the past year.

Wages in the city have risen by 5.71% over the past year, scoring 84 out of 100, which is faster than most other metros, meaning employers may face rising labor costs, while workers will see an increase in their paychecks. This rapid wage growth could be a challenge for businesses looking to keep costs under control, but it's a boon for workers who will have more purchasing power.

Cost of Living

How many hours a local worker needs to work to pay for one square foot of a home, and whether that's improving.

The cost of living in Portland-Vancouver-Hillsboro is relatively affordable, with a score of 61 out of 100, and a price per square foot of $302, compared to an hourly pay of $42.38, and housing prices are actually falling by 2.3% over the past year. This makes the city more attractive to workers and businesses looking for a relatively affordable place to live and operate, with housing costs becoming more manageable.

Labor Force Growth

Whether the number of people working or looking for work is growing.

The labor force in the city is shrinking, with a decline of 0.60% over the past year, scoring 38 out of 100, which means the pool of available workers is decreasing, making it harder for businesses to find new talent. This decline in the labor force could lead to a structural headwind for hiring and business growth in the city.

Building Permits

How many new homes builders got approval for, compared with a year ago.

New home approvals in Portland-Vancouver-Hillsboro have increased by 20.09% over the past year, scoring 78 out of 100, which signals that developers are confident in the city's future and are building more homes to meet demand. This increase in building permits is a positive sign for the city's housing market, indicating that the supply of homes will increase, making it easier for people to find a place to live.

Days on Market

How long a typical home for sale takes to find a buyer; about 35 to 80 days is healthiest.

Homes in the city are taking 71 days to sell, which is relatively slow, with a score of 30 out of 100, and this time is not changing much over the past year, indicating a softening housing market. This slower pace of home sales could be a sign that the housing market is weakening, making it easier for buyers to find a home, but potentially leading to lower prices and less demand.

Office Economy

The share of jobs in office-based fields like tech, finance and professional services.

The city's office-based workforce is relatively small, scoring 14 out of 100, which means it may not be the best fit for businesses in the tech, finance, or professional services sectors that rely on a deep pool of specialized talent. This smaller office economy makes the city less competitive for businesses that need a large and skilled workforce in these areas.

The bottom line is that Portland-Vancouver-Hillsboro's biggest strength is its rapid wage growth, which is attracting workers and driving economic activity, but its biggest risk is its slow job growth and shrinking labor force, which could limit business expansion and hiring in the city. Overall, the city's mixed economic signals make it a challenging but potentially rewarding location for businesses and workers looking for a relatively affordable place to live and operate.
