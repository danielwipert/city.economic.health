Economic Analysis

The Portland-Vancouver-Hillsboro metro area has an overall grade of C, ranking 35.7th percentile out of 50 US metros, with a composite score largely defined by its weak labor demand and low office economy share. The labor demand composite score of 2.28, combining a -1.44% employment growth rate and a -0.393% weekly hours deviation from trend, signals a contraction in labor demand. This, coupled with a low share of professional and office workers, characterizes the city's current economic challenges.

Labor Demand

The employment growth rate of -1.44% and weekly hours deviation of -0.393% from the city's own trend indicate a labor market that is contracting, with fewer jobs being added and hours worked below the trend. This combination signals a lack of genuine demand expansion, suggesting that the labor market is experiencing a contraction rather than a growth phase. The low labor demand composite score of 2.28 further emphasizes this point, ranking in the bottom tier at the 4th percentile.

Unemployment

The unemployment rate of 5.00% places the city below the average, ranking at the 24th percentile. This rate suggests that while the market is not extremely tight, it does have some slack, making it somewhat easier for businesses to hire compared to tighter labor markets. However, the practical implication for a business trying to hire here is that it may still face challenges in finding the right talent, albeit with less wage pressure than in tighter markets.

Wage Growth

The year-over-year wage growth rate of +5.27% is above average, ranking at the 74th percentile. This indicates that wages are rising faster than in most other cities, which can lead to higher labor costs for employers. On the other hand, it also means that workers have stronger purchasing power, which can be beneficial for local consumer demand.

Cost of Living

With a cost of living ratio of $306/sqft to $42.18/hr, and a PSF that is falling by -2.2% YoY, the city ranks above average in terms of affordability, at the 65th percentile. This means that the city is relatively more affordable compared to its peers, which can be a significant advantage for talent attraction without needing to offer wage premiums to compensate for high living costs.

Labor Force Growth

The civilian labor force is growing at a rate of -0.81% year-over-year, indicating that the labor force supply is contracting. This contraction implies a structural headwind for hiring, as the pool of potential workers is shrinking, making it more challenging for businesses to find and recruit talent.

Building Permits

The year-over-year change in residential building permits is +2.48%, which is near the median, ranking at the 56th percentile. This slight increase suggests that housing supply is slowly expanding, which can improve affordability and accommodate workforce growth in the future. However, the pace of this expansion is moderate, and thus, its impact on future affordability and workforce accommodation will be gradual.

Days on Market

The current median days on market is 57 days, with a year-over-year increase of +3.6%. This indicates a market that is slowing down, becoming more accessible for workers relocating to the city. The near-median ranking at the 46th percentile suggests that while homes are not selling as quickly as in hotter markets, the market is not excessively slow, providing a relatively balanced environment for both buyers and sellers.

Office Economy

The share of jobs in professional and office sectors is 0.68, ranking in the bottom tier at the 8th percentile. This indicates a relatively shallow talent pool in these sectors, making the city less suited for businesses that rely heavily on tech, finance, consulting, or HQ operations. Instead, the city's economy may be more conducive to industries that are less dependent on a deep professional and office worker base.

The Portland-Vancouver-Hillsboro metro area offers a business environment with above-average wage growth and a relatively affordable cost of living, which can be attractive for talent acquisition and retention. However, the single biggest risk or constraint for a decision-maker is the city's weak labor demand and contracting labor force, which can pose significant challenges for hiring and workforce expansion.
