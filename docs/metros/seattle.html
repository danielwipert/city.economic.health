Economic Analysis

The Seattle-Tacoma-Bellevue metro area has an overall grade of B, ranking at the 52.3th percentile among 50 US metros, with a composite score driven significantly by its top-tier wage growth and strong building permits expansion. The city's economic character is most defined by its +5.79% year-over-year wage growth and a +93.96% increase in building permits, signaling a dynamic labor market and expanding housing supply. These metrics suggest a city with a strong economy but also potential labor cost pressures.

Labor Demand

The employment growth rate in Seattle-Tacoma-Bellevue is +0.81% year-over-year, combined with a -0.048% deviation in weekly hours from its own trend, indicating a near median labor demand composite score of 5.39. This combination signals a moderate expansion in jobs but with hours worked slightly below trend, suggesting some genuine demand but not at an accelerated pace. The labor market is thus characterized by steady, albeit not spectacular, growth.

Unemployment

The unemployment rate in Seattle-Tacoma-Bellevue stands at 5.00%, placing it in the bottom tier at the 8th percentile, which means the labor market is relatively tight. This tightness implies that businesses may face challenges in hiring, potentially leading to upward pressure on wages. For a company looking to staff up, this market presents a significant recruitment challenge.

Wage Growth

With a year-over-year wage growth rate of +5.79%, Seattle-Tacoma-Bellevue is experiencing fast wage growth, ranking it in the top tier at the 84th percentile. This rapid wage growth suggests that labor costs for employers are rising quickly, which is good for worker purchasing power but may erode profit margins for businesses. Companies should factor in these increasing labor costs when considering relocation or expansion.

Cost of Living

The cost of living in Seattle-Tacoma-Bellevue, with a PSF of $432/sqft and average hourly earnings of $47.42, results in a ratio of 9.11, ranking near the median at the 41st percentile. This indicates that the city is neither exceptionally affordable nor overly expensive compared to its peers. The slight decrease in PSF (-1.8% YoY) is a positive sign for affordability. However, the city's cost of living does not offer a significant talent attraction advantage without wage premiums.

Labor Force Growth

The civilian labor force in Seattle-Tacoma-Bellevue has contracted by -0.77% year-over-year, indicating a shrinking labor pool. This contraction poses a structural headwind for hiring, as the supply of potential workers is decreasing. Businesses looking to expand their workforce may find it challenging due to this declining labor force growth.

Building Permits

The city has seen a significant increase of +93.96% in residential building permits year-over-year, signaling a rapid expansion in housing supply. This strong growth in building permits is a positive indicator for future affordability and suggests that the city is actively addressing potential housing shortages, which could improve its attractiveness for relocating workers.

Days on Market

Homes in Seattle-Tacoma-Bellevue currently sit on the market for a median of 45 days, with a year-over-year increase of +2.3%. This slight increase in days on market, ranking above average at the 62nd percentile, suggests a normalization of the housing market, making it somewhat more accessible for workers relocating to the city. However, the market remains relatively competitive.

Office Economy

With an office and professional worker share ranked above average at the 74th percentile, Seattle-Tacoma-Bellevue has a deep talent pool suited for tech, finance, consulting, and HQ decisions. This makes the city an attractive location for businesses in these sectors but less ideal for those in industrial or logistics-dominant economies.

In conclusion, Seattle-Tacoma-Bellevue offers a dynamic economy with strong wage growth and an expanding housing supply, making it an attractive location for certain types of businesses, especially those in professional and office sectors. However, the single biggest risk or constraint for decision-makers is the tight labor market and rapid wage growth, which could pose significant challenges for staffing and labor cost management.
