Economic Analysis

The Seattle-Tacoma-Bellevue metro area has an overall grade of B, ranking in the 54.8th percentile among 50 US metros, with a composite score driven largely by its strong wage growth and building permits expansion. The city's economic character is most defined by its top-tier wage growth of 5.79% year-over-year and its rapid expansion in building permits, up 93.96% year-over-year. These metrics suggest a city with a strong, growing economy, but also one with potential labor cost and affordability implications.

Labor Demand

The employment growth rate in Seattle-Tacoma-Bellevue is 0.81% year-over-year, combined with a weekly hours deviation of -0.048% from its own trend, indicating a near median labor demand composite score of 5.39. This combination signals a moderate expansion in jobs, but with hours worked slightly below trend, suggesting some degree of labor market slack. The labor demand is not as strong as in other metros, but it does indicate genuine demand expansion rather than contraction.

Unemployment

The unemployment rate in Seattle-Tacoma-Bellevue is 5.00%, ranking in the 20th percentile, which means the market has more slack than most other metros. This implies that businesses may find it easier to hire in this city compared to tighter labor markets, but it also suggests weaker local consumer demand due to the higher unemployment rate. For a business trying to hire, this could mean less competition for talent, but also potentially less skilled workers available.

Wage Growth

The year-over-year wage growth in Seattle-Tacoma-Bellevue is 5.79%, ranking in the top tier at the 84th percentile. This fast wage growth rate implies rising labor costs for employers, but it also means good news for worker purchasing power. As wages rise, businesses may face increasing costs, but the local workforce will have more disposable income to spend, potentially boosting local economic activity.

Cost of Living

Seattle-Tacoma-Bellevue has a cost of living score that ranks in the 39th percentile, with a PSF to earnings ratio of $438/sqft to $47.42/hr, or 9.24, and a year-over-year decrease in PSF of -2.2%. This means the city is less affordable than many of its peers, which could make it harder to attract talent without offering wage premiums. The falling PSF relative to wages is a positive sign, but the overall affordability score suggests that businesses may need to compensate employees for the higher cost of living.

Labor Force Growth

The civilian labor force in Seattle-Tacoma-Bellevue is contracting at a rate of -0.96% year-over-year, ranking in the 26th percentile. This negative growth rate indicates that the labor force supply is shrinking, which could pose a structural headwind for hiring in the future. Businesses may face challenges in finding skilled workers as the labor pool contracts, making it essential to plan for potential talent shortages.

Building Permits

The year-over-year change in building permits is 93.96%, ranking in the top tier at the 96th percentile. This rapid expansion in residential building permits signals that housing supply is likely to increase, which could improve affordability and make it easier for workers to relocate to the city. As housing supply expands, the city may become more attractive to potential employees, reducing the need for wage premiums to compensate for high living costs.

Days on Market

The median days on market for homes in Seattle-Tacoma-Bellevue is 44 days, with a year-over-year increase of 10.0%, ranking in the top tier at the 92nd percentile. This slower market means that homes are sitting longer before being sold, which could make it easier for relocating workers to find housing. However, this also indicates a less competitive market, which might suggest demand erosion if not accompanied by strong job growth.

Office Economy

The share of jobs in professional and office sectors in Seattle-Tacoma-Bellevue ranks in the 74th percentile, indicating a deep talent pool suited for tech, finance, consulting, and HQ decisions. This city is well-suited for businesses that rely on specialized, knowledge-economy workers, but it may be less ideal for industries with more industrial or logistics-oriented workforces.

In conclusion, Seattle-Tacoma-Bellevue offers businesses a strong, growing economy with a highly skilled workforce, driven by top-tier wage growth and an expanding housing supply. However, the single biggest risk or constraint for decision-makers is the city's less affordable cost of living, which may require businesses to offer wage premiums to attract and retain talent, potentially increasing labor costs.
