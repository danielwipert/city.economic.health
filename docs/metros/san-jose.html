Economic Analysis

The San Jose-Sunnyvale-Santa Clara metro area has an overall grade of B, ranking in the 54.7th percentile among 50 US metros, with a composite score driven largely by its top-tier labor demand and building permits. The city's economic character is defined by its strong employment growth of 1.34% year-over-year and a weekly hours deviation of +0.839% from its own trend, indicating genuine demand expansion. This combination of metrics suggests a city with a robust job market.

Labor Demand

The employment growth rate of 1.34% year-over-year, combined with a weekly hours deviation of +0.839% from its own trend, signals genuine demand expansion in the San Jose-Sunnyvale-Santa Clara metro area. This indicates that not only are more jobs being added, but existing workers are also working more hours, suggesting a strong and growing economy. The labor demand composite score of 7.01 places the city in the top tier, at the 90th percentile.

Unemployment

The unemployment rate in San Jose-Sunnyvale-Santa Clara is 4.10%, which places it at the 70th percentile, indicating a relatively tight labor market. This means that businesses may face challenges in hiring due to the limited availability of workers, potentially leading to upward pressure on wages. The tight market implies that companies may need to offer competitive salaries to attract top talent.

Wage Growth

The year-over-year wage growth in the San Jose-Sunnyvale-Santa Clara metro area is +0.51%, which is in the bottom tier, ranking at the 8th percentile. This slow wage growth suggests that labor costs for employers are not rising rapidly, but it also indicates weak bargaining power for workers, potentially affecting their purchasing power. The stagnant wage growth may be a concern for businesses relying on local consumer demand.

Cost of Living

San Jose-Sunnyvale-Santa Clara has a cost of living score that places it in the bottom tier, at the 2nd percentile, with a PSF of $811/sqft and average hourly earnings of $53.77/hr, resulting in a ratio of 15.08. The fact that PSF is falling by -1.3% year-over-year is a key driver of affordability. However, the low percentile rank indicates that the city is relatively expensive compared to its peers, making it challenging to attract talent without offering wage premiums.

Labor Force Growth

The civilian labor force in San Jose-Sunnyvale-Santa Clara is growing at a rate of -0.48% year-over-year, which is near the median, ranking at the 48th percentile. This negative growth rate indicates that the labor force supply is contracting, presenting a structural headwind for hiring and potentially limiting the city's ability to support rapid business expansion.

Building Permits

The year-over-year change in building permits is +120.21%, placing the city in the top tier, at the 98th percentile. This significant increase in residential building permits suggests that housing supply is expanding, which could improve affordability and accommodate a growing workforce in the future. The surge in permits is a positive sign for businesses concerned about the availability of housing for their employees.

Days on Market

The current median days on market in San Jose-Sunnyvale-Santa Clara is 36 days, with a year-over-year decrease of -10.0%. This rapid market, ranking at the 2nd percentile, indicates that homes are selling very quickly, making it challenging for relocating workers to find housing. The fast-paced market could be a concern for businesses trying to attract talent from other areas.

Office Economy

The share of jobs in professional and office sectors in San Jose-Sunnyvale-Santa Clara places it at the 70th percentile, indicating a deep talent pool suited for tech, finance, consulting, and HQ decisions. The city's economy is well-suited for knowledge-intensive industries but may not be as conducive to industrial or logistics-dominant businesses.

The San Jose-Sunnyvale-Santa Clara metro area offers businesses a strong labor market with genuine demand expansion, driven by top-tier employment growth and building permits. However, the single biggest risk or constraint for decision-makers is the city's relatively high cost of living, which may require businesses to offer wage premiums to attract and retain talent, potentially increasing labor costs.
