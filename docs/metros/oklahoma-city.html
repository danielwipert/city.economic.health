Economic Analysis

Oklahoma City has an overall grade of C+, with a score of 44.8 out of 100, indicating a fair economy compared to other metros. The city's economy is currently defined by its relatively slow job growth, with employment increasing by only 0.5% over the past year, and a moderate unemployment rate of 3.7%, which is about average for the 50 metros.

Labor Demand

Whether employers are adding jobs, based on job growth over the past year and changes in weekly work hours.

Oklahoma City is adding jobs, but at a slow pace, with employment growth of 0.5% over the past year, and workers' hours are actually falling by 1.1% compared to the same period last year. This means that the job market is not as strong as in other metros, with a score of 26 out of 100, indicating a weakness in labor demand.

Unemployment

The share of people who want a job but can't find one; lower is better.

The unemployment rate in Oklahoma City is 3.7%, which is about average for the 50 metros, making it neither particularly easy nor hard for employers to hire, with a score of 44 out of 100. This moderate unemployment rate suggests that employers may face some competition for workers, but it's not as intense as in metros with lower unemployment rates.

Wage Growth

How much average hourly pay rose over the past year.

Pay in Oklahoma City rose by 4.0% over the past year, which is about average for the 50 metros, with a score of 58 out of 100. This moderate wage growth means that employers may face some upward pressure on labor costs, but workers will also see their paychecks increase, which can boost consumer demand.

Cost of Living

How many hours a local worker needs to work to pay for one square foot of a home, and whether that's improving.

Housing in Oklahoma City is relatively affordable, with a price per square foot of $170 and an hourly pay of $33.63, earning a score of 67 out of 100, indicating that the city is more affordable than most metros. The fact that housing prices are not increasing faster than wages makes it easier for workers to afford homes, which is a strength for the city.

Labor Force Growth

Whether the number of people working or looking for work is growing.

The pool of workers in Oklahoma City is shrinking slightly, with a decline of 0.2% over the past year, which is about average for the 50 metros, with a score of 50 out of 100. This slow labor force growth means that employers may face some challenges in finding new workers, but it's not a major concern.

Building Permits

How many new homes builders got approval for, compared with a year ago.

New home approvals in Oklahoma City increased by 7.5% over the past year, which is a strength, with a score of 60 out of 100, indicating that the city is building more homes than most metros. This increase in building permits suggests that the city's housing supply will expand in the future, which can help keep housing prices affordable.

Days on Market

How long a typical home for sale takes to find a buyer; about 35 to 80 days is healthiest.

Homes in Oklahoma City are taking around 60 days to sell, which is getting slower, with a 9.1% increase in days on market over the past year, earning a score of 30 out of 100, indicating a weakness in the housing market. This slower pace of home sales suggests that the housing market is softening, which can make it harder for workers to relocate to the city.

Office Economy

The share of jobs in office-based fields like tech, finance and professional services.

Oklahoma City has a relatively small office-based workforce, with a score of 8 out of 100, indicating that the city is not as well-suited for businesses that rely on professional and technical services. This smaller office workforce means that the city may not be the best fit for companies in the tech, finance, or consulting sectors.

Oklahoma City's biggest strength is its relatively affordable housing, with a cost of living score of 67 out of 100, making it an attractive option for workers and families. However, the city's biggest risk is its slow labor demand, with a score of 26 out of 100, which can make it harder for employers to find workers and for the city to attract new businesses.
