Economic Analysis

Oklahoma City has an overall grade of C+ with a composite score ranking it 46.6th percentile out of 50 US metros. The city's economic character is most defined by its near-median labor demand composite score of 5.17 and its below-average labor force growth rate of -1.65% YoY. These metrics suggest a mixed economic environment, with some indicators pointing to stability and others to potential challenges.

Labor Demand

Oklahoma City's employment growth rate is +0.74% YoY, while weekly hours are -0.163% below trend. This combination signals a moderate expansion in jobs, but with hours worked slightly below the city's own 12-month baseline, indicating some degree of survivor squeeze where remaining workers may be absorbing the load of eliminated roles. The labor demand composite score of 5.17 is near the median, suggesting a relatively stable but not particularly strong labor market.

Unemployment

The unemployment rate in Oklahoma City is 3.90%, ranking near the median in terms of labor market tightness. This rate suggests that the market has some slack, making it slightly easier for businesses to hire compared to tighter labor markets. However, the practical implication for a business trying to hire here is that while it may not face extreme competition for talent, it still needs to offer competitive wages and benefits to attract the best candidates.

Wage Growth

Wage growth in Oklahoma City is +4.20% YoY, which is near the median in terms of pace. This moderate wage growth suggests that labor costs for employers are rising, but not at an alarming rate, and worker purchasing power is increasing, which can support local consumer demand. The implication for employers is a need to balance wage increases with productivity gains to maintain profitability.

Cost of Living

Oklahoma City has a cost of living score that ranks it near the median (57th percentile) in terms of affordability, with a PSF to earnings ratio of $171/sqft to $34.33/hr, or 4.98. This suggests that the city is relatively affordable compared to its peers, which can be a talent attraction advantage without needing significant wage premiums. The city's affordability can help businesses attract and retain workers without incurring high labor costs.

Labor Force Growth

The civilian labor force in Oklahoma City is shrinking at a rate of -1.65% YoY, indicating a contracting labor pool. This contraction poses a structural headwind for hiring, as businesses may face challenges in finding qualified workers to fill open positions. The implication for hiring capacity is that businesses may need to invest more in training and development or consider relocating workers from other areas.

Building Permits

Residential building permits in Oklahoma City are increasing by +14.19% YoY, signaling an expansion in future housing supply. This growth in permits suggests that developer confidence is strong, and the city is likely to see improvements in affordability and an easier time accommodating a growing workforce. The increase in housing supply can help mitigate potential workforce attraction and retention issues.

Days on Market

The median days on market for homes in Oklahoma City is 57 days, with a YoY increase of +9.6%. This indicates a slightly slower market, which can be more accessible for relocating workers. However, the relatively fast pace of home sales still suggests a competitive market, and workers may face some challenges in finding and securing housing quickly.

Office Economy

Oklahoma City's professional and office worker share ranks in the bottom tier (10th percentile), indicating a relatively shallow talent pool in these sectors. This suggests that the city is less suited for businesses requiring deep knowledge-economy talent, such as tech, finance, or consulting firms, but may be more appropriate for industries with different workforce needs, like logistics or manufacturing.

In conclusion, Oklahoma City offers a relatively affordable cost of living and a moderate labor market, which can be attractive for certain types of businesses. However, the single biggest risk or constraint for a decision-maker is the city's shrinking labor force, which poses challenges for hiring and workforce growth, and may require businesses to adapt their recruitment and retention strategies accordingly.
