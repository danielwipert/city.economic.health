Economic Analysis

The city of Jacksonville has an overall grade of C- with a composite score ranking it 31.9th percentile out of 50 US metros. The city's economic character is most defined by its low unemployment rate of 4.70% and its stagnant wage growth of -0.68% year-over-year, which signal a tight labor market with weak wage pressure. These metrics suggest that Jacksonville's economy is characterized by a scarcity of labor and limited upward mobility for workers.

Labor Demand

Jacksonville's employment growth rate is 0.33% year-over-year, while weekly hours are deviating -0.372% from the city's own 12-month baseline. This combination signals a contraction in labor demand, as the city is not adding jobs at a significant rate and hours worked are below trend. This suggests that businesses may not be expanding their operations or hiring new workers at a rapid pace.

Unemployment

The unemployment rate in Jacksonville is 4.70%, which is in the bottom tier of the percentile rankings, indicating a very tight labor market. This means that businesses trying to hire in this city will face significant competition for a limited pool of workers, leading to potential wage pressure and hiring challenges. With such a low unemployment rate, companies may need to offer higher wages or more attractive benefits to attract and retain talent.

Wage Growth

Wage growth in Jacksonville is -0.68% year-over-year, indicating stagnant or even declining wages. This slow wage growth means that employer labor costs are not rising rapidly, but it also implies that workers have limited bargaining power and may not have the purchasing power to drive local consumer demand. As a result, businesses may not see significant increases in labor costs, but they may also not benefit from a highly paid and consumer-driven local economy.

Cost of Living

Jacksonville has a cost of living score that ranks it 59th percentile, with a PSF of $217/sqft and average hourly earnings of $36.43/hr, resulting in a ratio of 5.96. This suggests that the city is near the median in terms of affordability, neither extremely expensive nor very affordable. The fact that PSF is falling -2.7% year-over-year is a positive sign for affordability. This means that businesses can attract talent without needing to offer significant wage premiums to offset the cost of living, as the city's affordability is relatively neutral compared to its peers.

Labor Force Growth

The civilian labor force in Jacksonville is growing at a rate of -0.15% year-over-year, indicating a slight contraction in the labor pool. This means that the supply of workers is not expanding, which could create structural headwinds for hiring and business expansion in the city. As the labor force is not growing, businesses may face challenges in finding new workers to fill open positions or support expansion plans.

Building Permits

Building permits in Jacksonville are increasing by 15.03% year-over-year, indicating an expansion in housing supply. This suggests that developer confidence is rising, and the future housing supply is likely to improve, which could lead to better affordability and easier workforce accommodation. As housing supply increases, the city may become more attractive to workers and businesses alike, potentially easing some of the labor market pressures.

Days on Market

The median days on market for homes in Jacksonville is 59 days, with a year-over-year decrease of -11.9%. This indicates a relatively fast-paced market where homes are selling quickly, which could make it challenging for relocating workers to find and secure housing. The fast pace of the market suggests that workers may need to act quickly to secure housing, and businesses may need to consider this when relocating employees or planning expansions.

Office Economy

Jacksonville's professional and office worker share ranks 54th percentile, indicating a near-median depth of talent pool. This suggests that the city is suited for businesses that require a moderate level of professional and office skills, but may not be the best fit for businesses requiring highly specialized or abundant tech, finance, or consulting talent. The city's office economy is neither extremely strong nor weak, making it a potential location for a variety of businesses, but perhaps not the most competitive for those requiring a deep pool of highly specialized workers.

The city of Jacksonville offers businesses a unique combination of a tight labor market and stagnant wage growth, which can be both a challenge and an opportunity. However, the single biggest risk or constraint for businesses considering this location is the limited labor pool and potential hiring challenges, which could hinder expansion plans or require significant investments in talent attraction and retention strategies.
