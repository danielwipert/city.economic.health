Economic Analysis

The Minneapolis-St. Paul-Bloomington metro area has an overall grade of C, ranking 37.2th percentile out of 50 US metros, with a composite score reflecting a mix of strengths and weaknesses. The city's economic character is most defined by its labor demand, with an above-average composite score of 5.63, and its stagnant wage growth, at just +0.01% year-over-year. These metrics suggest a city with some job market momentum but significant pressure on labor costs and worker purchasing power.

Labor Demand

The Minneapolis-St. Paul-Bloomington metro area has an employment growth rate of +1.46% year-over-year and a weekly hours deviation of -0.718% from its own trend, combining for an above-average labor demand composite score. This signals a genuine demand expansion, as jobs are being added and hours are running above trend, indicating a strong job market. The combination of these metrics suggests that businesses can expect to find a relatively robust labor market.

Unemployment

The unemployment rate in the Minneapolis-St. Paul-Bloomington metro area is 3.90%, ranking below average at the 34th percentile. This indicates a relatively tight labor market with some slack, making it moderately challenging for businesses to hire. As a result, businesses may face some wage pressure when trying to attract and retain talent in this market.

Wage Growth

The year-over-year wage growth in the Minneapolis-St. Paul-Bloomington metro area is +0.01%, ranking at the bottom tier of the 6th percentile. This stagnant wage growth suggests that labor costs for employers are not rising rapidly, but it also implies weak bargaining power for workers and limited growth in worker purchasing power. This could be a concern for businesses relying on local consumer demand.

Cost of Living

The Minneapolis-St. Paul-Bloomington metro area has a cost of living score that ranks below average at the 29th percentile, with a PSF of $212/sqft and average hourly earnings of $39.46, resulting in a ratio of 5.37. This suggests that the city is relatively expensive compared to its peers, which could make it challenging to attract talent without offering wage premiums. The fact that PSF is falling by -0.5% year-over-year slightly mitigates this, but the overall affordability remains a concern.

Labor Force Growth

The civilian labor force in the Minneapolis-St. Paul-Bloomington metro area is contracting at a rate of -1.38% year-over-year, indicating a shrinking labor pool. This contraction in labor force supply poses a structural headwind for hiring, as businesses may find it increasingly difficult to find the talent they need. This could limit the city's appeal for businesses looking to expand or relocate.

Building Permits

The Minneapolis-St. Paul-Bloomington metro area has seen a +9.15% year-over-year increase in residential building permits, indicating an expansion in housing supply. This growth in permits suggests that developer confidence is strong, and future housing supply is likely to improve, which could enhance affordability and make the city more attractive for workforce accommodation.

Days on Market

The median days on market for homes in the Minneapolis-St. Paul-Bloomington metro area is 37 days, with a year-over-year change of +0.0%, ranking below average at the 30th percentile. This relatively fast market means that homes are selling quickly, which could make it challenging for relocating workers to find housing. However, the stable year-over-year direction suggests a balanced market rather than an overheated one.

Office Economy

The Minneapolis-St. Paul-Bloomington metro area has a professional and office worker share that ranks at the bottom tier of the 14th percentile, indicating a relatively shallow talent pool in these sectors. This suggests that the city is less suited for businesses in tech, finance, consulting, or HQ decisions that require a deep knowledge-economy talent pool, but it may be more appropriate for industrial or logistics-dominant economies.

The Minneapolis-St. Paul-Bloomington metro area offers a mixed bag for businesses, with some strengths in labor demand and building permits, but significant challenges in wage growth, cost of living, and labor force contraction. The single biggest risk or constraint for decision-makers is the shrinking labor force, which could limit the city's appeal for businesses looking to expand or relocate, and necessitates careful consideration of talent attraction and retention strategies.
