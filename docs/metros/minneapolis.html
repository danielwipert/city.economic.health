Economic Analysis

The Minneapolis-St. Paul-Bloomington metro area has an overall grade of C, ranking 34.5th percentile out of 50 US metros, with a composite score driven largely by its labor demand and unemployment metrics. The city's labor demand composite score of 5.31, near the median, and an unemployment rate of 4.40%, below average, define its current economic character. Specifically, the employment growth rate of +1.36% and weekly hours deviation of -0.894% signal a mixed labor market.

Labor Demand

The employment growth rate of +1.36% and weekly hours deviation of -0.894% indicate a moderate expansion of jobs, but with hours worked below trend, suggesting some degree of survivor squeeze. This combination signals that while the city is adding jobs, the existing workforce is not seeing a significant increase in hours worked, which may impact productivity. The labor demand composite score of 5.31, near the median, further supports this assessment.

Unemployment

The unemployment rate of 4.40% is below average, ranking in the 30th percentile, indicating a relatively tight labor market. This means that businesses trying to hire in this city may face challenges in finding available workers, leading to potential wage pressure. With a tight market, companies may need to offer competitive salaries to attract top talent.

Wage Growth

The year-over-year wage growth rate of -0.14% is stagnant, ranking in the bottom tier. This slow wage growth environment means that labor costs for employers are not increasing significantly, but it also implies weak bargaining power for workers and potentially limited purchasing power. As a result, businesses may not face significant wage inflation, but workers may not have the disposable income to drive local consumer demand.

Cost of Living

The city's cost of living, with a PSF of $208/sqft and average hourly earnings of $40.22/hr, resulting in a ratio of 5.17, is near the median, ranking in the 45th percentile. This means that the city is relatively affordable compared to its peers, which can be a talent attraction advantage without requiring significant wage premiums. The fact that PSF is falling by -1.9% YoY further supports the city's affordability.

Labor Force Growth

The civilian labor force is contracting at a rate of -0.97% year-over-year, indicating a shrinking labor pool. This contraction implies a structural headwind for hiring, as the supply of available workers is decreasing. Businesses may need to consider strategies to attract workers from other areas or invest in workforce development programs.

Building Permits

The year-over-year change in residential building permits is +9.15%, indicating an expansion of housing supply. This increase in permits suggests that developer confidence is rising, and future housing supply is likely to improve, which can lead to better affordability and accommodation for the workforce. As a result, the city may become more attractive to workers and businesses alike.

Days on Market

The current median days on market is 38 days, with a year-over-year direction of +0.0%, indicating a relatively stable and accessible market for homebuyers. This means that workers relocating to this city may find it relatively easy to find and purchase a home, which can be a significant advantage for businesses trying to attract talent. However, the below-average percentile rank of 26th suggests that the market may still be somewhat competitive.

Office Economy

The share of jobs in professional and office sectors is 1.80, ranking in the 24th percentile, indicating a relatively shallow talent pool. This city is best suited for businesses that do not require a deep knowledge-economy talent pool, such as industrial or logistics-dominant companies. On the other hand, it may not be the ideal location for tech, finance, or consulting firms that rely heavily on specialized office workers.

The Minneapolis-St. Paul-Bloomington metro area offers businesses a relatively affordable cost of living and an expanding housing supply, which can be attractive for talent acquisition and retention. However, the single biggest risk or constraint for decision-makers is the shrinking labor pool, which may limit hiring capacity and require businesses to invest in workforce development or attraction strategies to remain competitive.
