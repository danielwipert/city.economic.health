Economic Analysis

The Minneapolis-St. Paul-Bloomington metro area has an overall grade of C- with a composite score ranking it at the 29.9th percentile out of 50 US metros. This city's economic character is most defined by its low wage growth rate of +0.54% year-over-year and its below-average labor force growth rate of -1.38% year-over-year, indicating a challenging environment for businesses looking to expand or relocate. The combination of these metrics suggests a city with limited labor market dynamism.

Labor Demand

The employment growth rate in Minneapolis-St. Paul-Bloomington is +0.27% year-over-year, while weekly hours are deviating -0.495% from the city's own 12-month baseline. This combination signals a labor market that is not experiencing genuine demand expansion, as the hours worked are below trend despite modest job growth. The labor demand composite score of 4.18 ranks near the median at the 44th percentile, indicating a lackluster labor market.

Unemployment

The unemployment rate in Minneapolis-St. Paul-Bloomington is 3.90%, ranking below average at the 34th percentile. This indicates a labor market with some slack, making it slightly easier for businesses to hire compared to tighter markets. However, the relatively low unemployment rate still suggests that businesses may face some competition for talent and upward pressure on wages.

Wage Growth

The year-over-year wage growth rate in Minneapolis-St. Paul-Bloomington is +0.54%, ranking at the bottom tier of the 10th percentile. This stagnant wage growth rate implies that labor costs for employers are not rising rapidly, but it also means that worker purchasing power is not increasing significantly. This could have implications for businesses relying on local consumer demand.

Cost of Living

Minneapolis-St. Paul-Bloomington has a cost of living score that ranks below average at the 33rd percentile, with a PSF of $212/sqft and average hourly earnings of $39.59/hr, resulting in a ratio of 5.35. The PSF is falling by -0.5% year-over-year, which is a positive sign for affordability. This suggests that the city is relatively expensive, but the falling PSF could make it more attractive for talent without requiring significant wage premiums.

Labor Force Growth

The civilian labor force in Minneapolis-St. Paul-Bloomington is contracting at a rate of -1.38% year-over-year. This indicates that the labor pool is shrinking, presenting a structural headwind for businesses looking to hire and expand in the area. The implication is that businesses may face challenges in finding the talent they need to grow.

Building Permits

The year-over-year change in residential building permits in Minneapolis-St. Paul-Bloomington is -11.74%, indicating a tightening of the housing supply. This signals that future affordability and workforce accommodation may be at risk, as the supply of new housing is not keeping pace with demand. This could lead to increased competition for housing and upward pressure on prices.

Days on Market

The median days on market for homes in Minneapolis-St. Paul-Bloomington is 37 days, with a year-over-year change of +0.0%. This suggests a relatively stable housing market that is neither extremely competitive nor extremely accessible for relocating workers. The days on market rank below average at the 30th percentile, indicating a market that is slightly slower than peer cities.

Office Economy

The share of jobs in professional and office sectors in Minneapolis-St. Paul-Bloomington ranks at the bottom tier of the 12th percentile, indicating a relatively shallow talent pool in these areas. This suggests that the city is less suited for businesses in the tech, finance, consulting, or HQ sectors, which rely on a deep knowledge-economy talent pool. Instead, the city may be more attractive to businesses in industrial or logistics-dominant sectors.

The Minneapolis-St. Paul-Bloomington metro area offers businesses a relatively stable labor market with some slack, but it also presents challenges such as stagnant wage growth, a shrinking labor pool, and a tightening housing supply. The single biggest risk or constraint for businesses considering this location is the limited labor market dynamism, which could hinder growth and expansion plans.
