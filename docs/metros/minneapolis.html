Economic Analysis

The Minneapolis-St. Paul-Bloomington metro area has an overall grade of C, ranking 36.3th percentile out of 50 US metros, with a composite score reflecting a mix of mediocre labor demand and cost of living. The city's economic character is most defined by its near-median labor demand composite score of 5.31 and its bottom-tier wage growth of -0.14% year-over-year. These metrics suggest a city with limited job market momentum and stagnant wages.

Labor Demand

The employment growth rate in Minneapolis-St. Paul-Bloomington is +1.36% year-over-year, while weekly hours are -0.894% below the city's own trend. This combination signals a lack of genuine demand expansion, as the modest job growth is not accompanied by increased hours worked, suggesting some level of labor market slack. The labor demand composite score of 5.31 is near the median, indicating a neutral labor market condition.

Unemployment

The unemployment rate in Minneapolis-St. Paul-Bloomington is 4.40%, ranking below average at the 30th percentile. This indicates a labor market with some slack, making it relatively easier for businesses to hire compared to tighter markets. However, the practical implication is that local consumer demand may be weaker due to the higher unemployment rate.

Wage Growth

The year-over-year wage growth in Minneapolis-St. Paul-Bloomington is -0.14%, ranking at the bottom tier. This stagnant wage growth signals a flat labor cost environment for employers, but it also means weak bargaining power for workers and limited growth in worker purchasing power. The implication is that businesses may not face significant wage pressure, but the local workforce may not have strong disposable income.

Cost of Living

Minneapolis-St. Paul-Bloomington has a cost of living ratio of $208/sqft to $40.22/hr, with a percentile rank of 45th, indicating a near-median affordability level. The city's PSF is falling by -1.9% year-over-year, which is a positive factor for affordability. This means that the city may not have a significant talent attraction advantage without offering wage premiums, as its affordability is average compared to peers.

Labor Force Growth

The civilian labor force in Minneapolis-St. Paul-Bloomington is contracting at a rate of -0.97% year-over-year, ranking below average at the 24th percentile. This indicates a shrinking labor pool, which poses a structural headwind for hiring and business expansion in the city. The implication is that businesses may face challenges in finding and retaining talent in the long term.

Building Permits

The year-over-year change in residential building permits in Minneapolis-St. Paul-Bloomington is +31.92%, ranking at the top tier. This significant increase in permits signals an expansion in future housing supply, which should improve affordability and accommodate workforce growth. The implication is that the city is likely to become more attractive for relocating workers and businesses due to the anticipated improvement in housing affordability.

Days on Market

The current median days on market for homes in Minneapolis-St. Paul-Bloomington is 38 days, with a year-over-year change of +0.0%, ranking below average at the 26th percentile. This indicates a relatively fast-paced housing market, which may be competitive for relocating workers. The implication is that businesses may need to consider the potential challenges their employees could face in finding and securing housing in the city.

Office Economy

Minneapolis-St. Paul-Bloomington has an office economy percentile rank of 24th, indicating a relatively shallow professional talent pool. The city is less suited for businesses that require a deep knowledge-economy talent pool, such as tech, finance, or consulting firms, but may be more appropriate for industrial or logistics-dominant businesses. The implication is that businesses with specialized office sector needs may find the city less attractive due to its limited talent pool.

The Minneapolis-St. Paul-Bloomington metro area offers businesses a mix of average affordability and a relatively slack labor market, but the single biggest risk or constraint is the stagnant wage growth and shrinking labor force, which may limit the city's attractiveness for businesses requiring a skilled and growing workforce. The city's expanding housing supply is a positive factor, but the competitive housing market and limited office economy talent pool are drawbacks that decision-makers should carefully consider. Overall, the city's mediocre economic character suggests that businesses should weigh these factors carefully before making a location decision.
