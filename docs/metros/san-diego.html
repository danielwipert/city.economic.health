Economic Analysis

The San Diego-Chula Vista-Carlsbad metro area has an overall grade of C, ranking 38.7th percentile out of 50 US metros, with a composite score driven largely by its high wage growth and low unemployment rate, which are 7.50% and 4.90%, respectively. The city's economic character is defined by these two metrics, indicating a tight labor market with rising labor costs. Specifically, the wage growth rate of 7.50% and unemployment rate of 4.90% suggest a competitive job market.

Labor Demand

The employment growth rate in San Diego-Chula Vista-Carlsbad is 0.70% year-over-year, combined with a weekly hours deviation of +0.152% from its own trend, signaling a genuine demand expansion. This combination indicates that the city is experiencing modest job growth, with hours worked above the trend, suggesting that employers are increasing their workforce and workload. The labor demand composite score of 5.43 corresponds to a near-median percentile rank of 52nd.

Unemployment

The unemployment rate in San Diego-Chula Vista-Carlsbad is 4.90%, which corresponds to a below-average percentile rank of 30th, indicating a relatively tight labor market. This means that businesses may face challenges in hiring, as the pool of available workers is smaller, and may need to offer competitive wages to attract talent. The tight labor market implies upward pressure on wages.

Wage Growth

The year-over-year wage growth in San Diego-Chula Vista-Carlsbad is 7.50%, which is a top-tier growth rate, ranking in the 94th percentile. This rapid wage growth suggests that labor costs for employers are rising quickly, while workers are experiencing increased purchasing power. The high wage growth rate may be a challenge for businesses looking to control labor costs.

Cost of Living

San Diego-Chula Vista-Carlsbad has a cost of living score that corresponds to a bottom-tier percentile rank of 12th, with a price-to-salary ratio of $587/sqft to $44.28/hr, or 13.26. Although the PSF is falling by 3.5% year-over-year, the city remains relatively expensive compared to its peers. This means that businesses may need to offer wage premiums to attract and retain talent, as the high cost of living may offset the city's other advantages.

Labor Force Growth

The civilian labor force in San Diego-Chula Vista-Carlsbad is shrinking at a rate of -2.06% year-over-year, indicating a contracting labor pool. This decline in labor force growth suggests that the city may face structural headwinds in hiring, as the supply of available workers is decreasing. Businesses may need to adapt their hiring strategies to this shrinking labor pool.

Building Permits

The number of residential building permits in San Diego-Chula Vista-Carlsbad has decreased by 36.38% year-over-year, indicating a tightening housing supply. This sharp decline in building permits signals that the city's future affordability and workforce accommodation may be at risk, as the supply of new housing is not keeping pace with demand. This may exacerbate the city's existing affordability challenges.

Days on Market

The median days on market for homes in San Diego-Chula Vista-Carlsbad is 46 days, with a year-over-year decrease of 6.1%. This relatively fast pace of home sales suggests that the city's housing market is competitive, and relocating workers may face challenges in finding available housing. The low days on market percentile rank of 4th indicates a hot market.

Office Economy

San Diego-Chula Vista-Carlsbad has a professional and office worker share that corresponds to a near-median percentile rank of 52nd, with a composite score of 2.58. This suggests that the city has a moderately deep talent pool in professional and office sectors, making it suitable for businesses in tech, finance, and consulting. However, it may be less suited for industries with more industrial or logistics-oriented workforces.

The San Diego-Chula Vista-Carlsbad metro area offers businesses a highly competitive labor market with rising wages, but also presents significant challenges in terms of affordability and labor supply. The single biggest risk or constraint for decision-makers is the city's extremely low ranking in building permits, which threatens to exacerbate the existing affordability crisis and limit the city's ability to accommodate a growing workforce.
