Economic Analysis

The Chicago-Naperville-Elgin metro area has an overall grade of C- with a composite score ranking it at the 27.2th percentile out of 50 US metros. This city's economic character is most defined by its labor force growth rate of -2.46% YoY and its building permits decline of -42.35% YoY, indicating significant structural headwinds for hiring and future affordability. The combination of these metrics suggests a challenging environment for businesses looking to expand or relocate.

Labor Demand

The employment growth rate in Chicago-Naperville-Elgin is +0.17% YoY, and weekly hours are deviating +0.674% from the city's own 12-month baseline, resulting in a labor demand composite score of 5.21, which ranks near the median at the 46th percentile. This combination signals a genuine demand expansion, albeit a modest one. The modest growth in jobs and hours worked above trend suggests that the labor market is experiencing some level of expansion.

Unemployment

The unemployment rate in Chicago-Naperville-Elgin is 4.90%, ranking below average at the 22nd percentile. This indicates a relatively tight labor market with some slack, making it moderately challenging for businesses to hire. The practical implication for a business trying to hire here is that they may face some competition for talent and potentially rising wage pressure.

Wage Growth

The year-over-year wage growth rate in Chicago-Naperville-Elgin is +3.11%, ranking near the median at the 44th percentile. This represents a moderate pace of wage growth, which implies rising labor costs for employers and increasing worker purchasing power. As wages continue to grow, businesses may need to adjust their compensation packages to remain competitive.

Cost of Living

Chicago-Naperville-Elgin has a cost of living score that ranks below average at the 22nd percentile, with a PSF of $221/sqft and average hourly earnings of $37.34/hr, resulting in a ratio of 5.92. This means the city is relatively expensive compared to its peers, which could make it challenging to attract talent without offering wage premiums. The 3.8% YoY increase in PSF further exacerbates the affordability issue.

Labor Force Growth

The civilian labor force in Chicago-Naperville-Elgin is contracting at a rate of -2.46% YoY, indicating a shrinking labor pool. This contraction implies a structural headwind for hiring, as the supply of potential workers is decreasing. Businesses may need to consider alternative locations or strategies to mitigate this risk.

Building Permits

The number of building permits in Chicago-Naperville-Elgin has declined by -42.35% YoY, signaling a tightening of the housing supply. This sharp decline suggests that future affordability and workforce accommodation may be at risk, as the supply of new housing units is not keeping pace with demand. This could lead to increased competition for existing housing stock and rising costs.

Days on Market

The current median days on market in Chicago-Naperville-Elgin is 36 days, with a YoY decline of -2.7%. This relatively fast pace of home sales indicates a competitive market, which may make it challenging for relocating workers to find affordable housing. The declining days on market suggests that the market is still favoring sellers, making it tough for buyers.

Office Economy

The share of professional and office workers in Chicago-Naperville-Elgin ranks below average at the 38th percentile, with a composite score of 2.22. This suggests a moderately deep talent pool, making the city more suited for businesses that are not heavily reliant on specialized office or professional roles. The city may be less competitive for tech, finance, or consulting businesses that require a deep knowledge-economy talent pool.

The Chicago-Naperville-Elgin metro area offers businesses a moderately growing labor market with modest wage growth, but it is constrained by a shrinking labor force, declining building permits, and a relatively expensive cost of living. The single biggest risk or constraint for decision-makers is the city's limited labor force growth and tightening housing supply, which may lead to increased competition for talent and rising costs.
