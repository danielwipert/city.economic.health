Economic Analysis

The Chicago-Naperville-Elgin metro area has an overall grade of C- with a composite score ranking it 28.6th percentile out of 50 US metros. The city's economic character is most defined by its high unemployment rate of 5.30% and its stagnant labor force growth, with a year-over-year change of -0.53%. These metrics signal a challenging labor market with limited expansion opportunities.

Labor Demand

The employment growth rate in Chicago-Naperville-Elgin is +0.17% year-over-year, combined with a +0.674% deviation in weekly hours from its own 12-month baseline. This combination signals a genuine demand expansion, albeit a slow one, as hours are running above trend during a period of job growth. However, the labor demand composite score of 5.21 is near the median, indicating a moderate level of job market activity.

Unemployment

The unemployment rate in Chicago-Naperville-Elgin is 5.30%, ranking it in the bottom tier at the 8th percentile. This indicates a labor market with significant slack, making it easier for businesses to hire workers without facing intense competition. However, this also implies weaker local consumer demand, which could impact businesses reliant on local spending.

Wage Growth

The year-over-year wage growth in Chicago-Naperville-Elgin is +3.11%, ranking it near the median at the 44th percentile. This moderate wage growth rate suggests that labor costs for employers are rising, but not excessively so. At the same time, workers are experiencing some improvement in their purchasing power, which could benefit businesses that rely on local consumer spending.

Cost of Living

Chicago-Naperville-Elgin has a cost of living score that ranks it in the 26th percentile, with a PSF of $221/sqft and an average hourly earnings of $37.34/hr, resulting in a ratio of 5.92. This indicates that the city is relatively expensive compared to its peers, which could make it challenging to attract talent without offering wage premiums. The fact that PSF is increasing by 2.8% year-over-year further exacerbates the affordability issue.

Labor Force Growth

The civilian labor force in Chicago-Naperville-Elgin is contracting at a rate of -0.53% year-over-year. This decline in labor force supply signals a structural headwind for hiring, as businesses may face challenges in finding qualified workers to fill open positions. The implication is that businesses may need to invest more in recruitment and training to attract and retain talent.

Building Permits

The number of residential building permits in Chicago-Naperville-Elgin has decreased by 28.57% year-over-year. This sharp decline in permits suggests that housing supply is tightening, which could lead to future affordability issues and make it more difficult for workers to relocate to the area. This, in turn, could impact businesses' ability to attract and retain talent.

Days on Market

The current median days on market in Chicago-Naperville-Elgin is 35 days, with a year-over-year decrease of 2.8%. This indicates a relatively fast-paced market, which could make it challenging for relocating workers to find housing. However, the fact that days on market are decreasing suggests that the market is still competitive, and businesses may need to offer relocation assistance to attract talent.

Office Economy

Chicago-Naperville-Elgin has an office economy score that ranks it in the 38th percentile, with a share of professional and office workers of 2.22. This indicates a moderate depth of professional talent pool, making the city suited for businesses that require a mix of office and non-office workers. However, it may not be the best fit for businesses that require a highly specialized or deep knowledge-economy talent pool.

The Chicago-Naperville-Elgin metro area offers businesses a labor market with some slack, making it easier to hire workers, but also poses challenges in terms of labor force growth and affordability. The single biggest risk or constraint for businesses considering this location is the potential difficulty in attracting and retaining talent due to the relatively high cost of living and tightening housing supply.
