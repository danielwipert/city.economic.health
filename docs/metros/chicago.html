Economic Analysis

The Chicago-Naperville-Elgin metro area has an overall grade of D, with a score of 22.5 out of 100, indicating significant economic challenges. The city's economy is currently defined by weak job growth, with employment declining by 0.1% over the past year, and a relatively high unemployment rate of 4.4%, which is higher than most of the 50 metros.

Labor Demand

Whether employers are adding jobs, based on job growth over the past year and changes in weekly work hours.

The city is losing jobs, with a 0.1% decline in employment over the past year, and while workers' hours are rising by 0.7%, this is still a weakness, indicating that the job market is not strong. This means that employers may not be hiring as aggressively as they are in other metros, with a score of 14, which is lower than most of the 50 metros.

Unemployment

The share of people who want a job but can't find one; lower is better.

The unemployment rate of 4.4% makes it relatively harder for employers to hire, as it is higher than most of the 50 metros, with a score of 20. This higher unemployment rate means that employers may have a larger pool of potential workers to choose from, but it also indicates weaker local consumer demand.

Wage Growth

How much average hourly pay rose over the past year.

Pay rose by 3.2% over the past year, which is slower than most of the 50 metros, with a score of 34, indicating that employers' labor costs are not rising as quickly as they are in other areas. This slower wage growth means that workers may not have as much bargaining power, and employers may not face as much pressure to increase wages.

Cost of Living

How many hours a local worker needs to work to pay for one square foot of a home, and whether that's improving.

Housing in the Chicago-Naperville-Elgin metro area is relatively expensive, with a price per square foot of $219 and hourly pay of $37.37, and it is getting even more expensive, with prices rising by 3.8% over the past year, earning a score of 20. This means that local workers may struggle to afford housing, and employers may need to offer higher wages to attract and retain talent.

Labor Force Growth

Whether the number of people working or looking for work is growing.

The pool of workers in the Chicago-Naperville-Elgin metro area is shrinking, with a decline of 2.0% over the past year, which is slower than most of the 50 metros, with a score of 12. This decline in the labor force means that employers may face a structural headwind in hiring, as there are fewer workers available to fill open positions.

Building Permits

How many new homes builders got approval for, compared with a year ago.

New home approvals declined by 5.0% over the past year, which is about average for the 50 metros, with a score of 44. This decline in building permits may signal that the future housing supply will be limited, which could put upward pressure on housing prices and make it even harder for workers to afford housing.

Days on Market

How long a typical home for sale takes to find a buyer; about 35 to 80 days is healthiest.

The typical home in the Chicago-Naperville-Elgin metro area takes 37 days to sell, which is getting slightly faster, with a decline of 2.6% over the past year, but this is still a weakness, with a score of 12. This faster pace of home sales may indicate a relatively tight housing market, which could make it difficult for relocating workers to find a home.

Office Economy

The share of jobs in office-based fields like tech, finance and professional services.

The Chicago-Naperville-Elgin metro area has an office-based workforce that is about average in size compared to other metros, with a score of 42, which suits businesses in the tech, finance, and professional services sectors. This means that the city has a deep talent pool in these areas, making it a good location for businesses that rely on these types of workers.

The bottom line is that the Chicago-Naperville-Elgin metro area's biggest strength is its office-based workforce, which is well-suited for businesses in the tech, finance, and professional services sectors. However, the city's biggest risk is its weak job growth and high unemployment rate, which may make it difficult for employers to attract and retain talent, and for workers to find employment.
