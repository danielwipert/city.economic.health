Economic Analysis

The Milwaukee-Waukesha metro area has an overall grade of B- with a composite score ranking it 44.3th percentile out of 50 US metros. This city's economic character is most defined by its below-average labor demand, with a composite score of 4.17, and its high unemployment rate of 3.80%, which ranks it in the 68th percentile. The labor demand and unemployment metrics suggest a city with a mix of challenges and opportunities for businesses.

Labor Demand

The employment growth rate in Milwaukee-Waukesha is -0.09% year-over-year, and weekly hours are deviating -1.171% from the city's own 12-month baseline. This combination signals a contraction in labor demand, indicating that the city is not experiencing genuine demand expansion. The negative employment growth rate and below-trend hours suggest that businesses may not be adding jobs at a pace that would drive economic growth.

Unemployment

The unemployment rate in Milwaukee-Waukesha is 3.80%, which is relatively low and ranks in the 68th percentile. This suggests a tight labor market with some slack, making it moderately challenging for businesses to hire new employees. As a result, businesses may face upward pressure on wages to attract and retain top talent in this market.

Wage Growth

The year-over-year wage growth rate in Milwaukee-Waukesha is +1.46%, which is relatively slow and ranks in the 16th percentile. This stagnant wage growth suggests that labor costs for employers are not rising rapidly, but it also implies that workers may have limited bargaining power. As a result, businesses may not face significant pressure to increase wages, but workers may not have the purchasing power to drive local consumer demand.

Cost of Living

Milwaukee-Waukesha has a cost of living score that ranks in the 18th percentile, with a PSF of $227/sqft and average hourly earnings of $36.01/hr, resulting in a ratio of 6.30. This suggests that the city is relatively expensive compared to its peers, making it challenging to attract talent without offering wage premiums. The rising PSF and stagnant wages contribute to the city's low affordability score.

Labor Force Growth

The civilian labor force in Milwaukee-Waukesha is growing at a rate of +1.66% year-over-year, which is a positive sign for businesses looking to hire. This expanding labor force supply suggests that the city has a growing pool of potential employees, making it easier for businesses to find the talent they need. As a result, businesses may face less competition for workers and have a larger pool of candidates to choose from.

Building Permits

The number of residential building permits in Milwaukee-Waukesha is increasing by +9.94% year-over-year, which suggests that housing supply is expanding. This growth in building permits signals that the city is likely to have a more affordable and accommodating housing market in the future, making it easier for workers to relocate and for businesses to attract talent.

Days on Market

The median days on market for homes in Milwaukee-Waukesha is currently 35 days, with a year-over-year increase of +16.7%. This suggests that the housing market is slowing down, making it more accessible for workers relocating to the city. The rising days on market indicate a more competitive market for buyers, which could be beneficial for workers looking to purchase homes.

Office Economy

Milwaukee-Waukesha has an office economy score that ranks in the 30th percentile, indicating a relatively shallow professional talent pool. This suggests that the city is best suited for businesses that do not require a deep pool of specialized office workers, such as industrial or logistics companies. However, it may not be the best fit for businesses that require a large number of professional or office workers, such as tech or finance companies.

The Milwaukee-Waukesha metro area offers businesses a mix of challenges and opportunities, with a growing labor force and expanding housing supply. However, the city's below-average labor demand and relatively expensive cost of living may pose significant risks for businesses looking to establish or expand operations in the area. The single biggest constraint for decision-makers is the city's tight labor market and stagnant wage growth, which may limit the availability of skilled workers and drive up labor costs.
