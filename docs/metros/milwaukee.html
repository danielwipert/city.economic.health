Economic Analysis

The Milwaukee-Waukesha metro area has an overall grade of C, ranking 38th out of 50 US metros, with a composite score defined largely by its low labor demand and stagnant wage growth, scoring 22nd and 6th percentile, respectively. The labor demand composite score of 3.95 and wage growth of +0.52% year-over-year are key metrics that characterize this city's current economic landscape. With an employment growth rate of -0.25% and weekly hours deviation of -1.588%, the city's labor market is experiencing contraction.

Labor Demand

The employment growth rate of -0.25% and weekly hours deviation of -1.588% indicate a contraction in labor demand, signaling that the city is experiencing a decline in job creation and a decrease in working hours. This combination suggests that the labor market is not expanding, and businesses may face challenges in finding skilled workers. The labor demand composite score of 3.95, ranking 22nd percentile, further reinforces this notion.

Unemployment

The unemployment rate of 3.80% places the city near the median, ranking 54th percentile, indicating a relatively balanced labor market with neither significant tightness nor slack. For businesses trying to hire, this means that while it may not be extremely difficult to find workers, there is also not an oversupply of labor, which could lead to moderate wage pressure. The unemployment rate suggests that the labor market is relatively stable.

Wage Growth

The year-over-year wage growth rate of +0.52% is stagnant, ranking 6th percentile, indicating that labor costs for employers are not rising rapidly, but worker purchasing power is also not increasing significantly. This slow wage growth suggests that businesses may not face immediate pressure to increase salaries, but it also implies that workers may not have strong bargaining power. The stagnant wage growth has implications for both employers and workers.

Cost of Living

The city's cost of living, with a PSF of $228/sqft and an earnings ratio of $35.76/hr, resulting in a ratio of 6.38, ranks 6th percentile, indicating that the city is relatively expensive. The 2.2% year-over-year increase in PSF further exacerbates the affordability issue. This means that the city may struggle to attract talent without offering wage premiums, as the high cost of living may deter potential employees.

Labor Force Growth

The civilian labor force is growing at a rate of +1.57% year-over-year, ranking 90th percentile, indicating that the workforce supply is expanding. This growth in labor force supply suggests that businesses may have an easier time finding skilled workers, as the pool of potential employees is increasing. The implication is that hiring capacity is improving.

Building Permits

The year-over-year change in building permits is +13.52%, ranking 58th percentile, indicating that housing supply is expanding. This increase in building permits suggests that developer confidence is rising, and future housing supply is likely to improve, which could lead to better affordability and workforce accommodation. The expanding housing supply is a positive sign for the city's future.

Days on Market

The current median days on market is 37 days, with a year-over-year increase of +12.1%, ranking 88th percentile, indicating a slower market. This means that homes are sitting on the market for longer, making it more accessible for workers relocating to the city. The slower market could be beneficial for individuals looking to buy or rent homes.

Office Economy

The share of professional and office workers is 2.06, ranking 30th percentile, indicating a relatively shallow talent pool. This suggests that the city is not well-suited for businesses that require a deep knowledge-economy talent pool, such as tech or finance companies. However, it may be more suitable for industries with fewer specialized roles, such as logistics or manufacturing.

The Milwaukee-Waukesha metro area offers businesses a relatively stable labor market and expanding workforce supply, but it is constrained by low labor demand and stagnant wage growth. The single biggest risk or constraint for decision-makers is the city's relatively high cost of living, which may require businesses to offer wage premiums to attract talent. Overall, the city's economic character is defined by its challenges in labor demand and affordability.
