Economic Analysis

The Milwaukee-Waukesha metro area has an overall grade of C-, with a score of 34.9 out of 100, indicating it is weaker than most of the 50 metros, with employment growth of only 0.2% over the past year being a significant concern. The city's economy is currently defined by its slow job growth and limited labor demand, with weekly hours worked falling by 1.9% compared to its own 12-month average.

Labor Demand

Whether employers are adding jobs, based on job growth over the past year and changes in weekly work hours.

The city is adding jobs at a rate of 0.2% over the past year, but workers' hours are falling by 1.9%, indicating a weak labor market with limited demand for workers. This means that the job market is not strong, with employers not hiring as many workers as other metros, and those who are working are putting in fewer hours.

Unemployment

The share of people who want a job but can't find one; lower is better.

The unemployment rate in Milwaukee-Waukesha is 3.2%, which is lower than most metros, scoring 68 out of 100, making it harder for employers to hire as there are fewer workers available. This low unemployment rate means that employers may face wage pressure and have to compete more aggressively to attract and retain workers.

Wage Growth

How much average hourly pay rose over the past year.

Pay rose by -0.2% over the past year, which is slower than most metros, scoring 4 out of 100, meaning that employers' labor costs are not rising as quickly, but workers' paychecks are not growing either. This slow wage growth may make it harder for workers to afford the rising cost of living in the area, and could impact their purchasing power.

Cost of Living

How many hours a local worker needs to work to pay for one square foot of a home, and whether that's improving.

The cost of living in Milwaukee-Waukesha is relatively high, with a price per square foot of $227 and hourly pay of $36.28, scoring 8 out of 100, indicating it is less affordable than most metros, and getting worse with a 2.7% increase in price per square foot over the past year. This means that housing is becoming less affordable for local workers, which could make it harder for employers to attract and retain talent.

Labor Force Growth

Whether the number of people working or looking for work is growing.

The pool of workers in Milwaukee-Waukesha is growing at a rate of 0.4% over the past year, which is faster than most metros, scoring 62 out of 100, making it easier for employers to find workers. This growing labor force means that employers have a larger pool of potential workers to hire from, which could help to drive business growth.

Building Permits

How many new homes builders got approval for, compared with a year ago.

New home approvals fell by 4.2% over the past year, scoring 48 out of 100, which is about average for the 50 metros, signaling that the future housing supply may not be growing as quickly as needed. This could lead to a shortage of housing options for workers, making it harder for employers to attract and retain talent.

Days on Market

How long a typical home for sale takes to find a buyer; about 35 to 80 days is healthiest.

Homes in Milwaukee-Waukesha are taking 37 days to sell, which is getting slower with a 15.6% increase over the past year, scoring 96 out of 100, indicating a healthier housing market than most metros. This means that the housing market is balanced, with buyers having enough time to find a home, and sellers able to sell their properties relatively quickly.

Office Economy

The share of jobs in office-based fields like tech, finance and professional services.

The city's office-based workforce is about average, scoring 46 out of 100, making it suitable for a range of businesses, including tech, finance, and professional services. This means that Milwaukee-Waukesha has a deep enough talent pool to support businesses that require specialized office workers, but may not be as strong as other metros in this regard.

The biggest strength of the Milwaukee-Waukesha metro area is its relatively low unemployment rate, making it an attractive location for businesses looking for a talented and available workforce. However, the city's biggest risk is its slow labor demand and limited job growth, which could make it harder for businesses to attract and retain workers, and ultimately impact the local economy.
