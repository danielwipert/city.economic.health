Economic Analysis

The Miami-Fort Lauderdale-West Palm Beach metro area has an overall grade of B- with a composite score ranking at the 53.9th percentile among 50 US metros. This city's economic character is most defined by its high wage growth rate of 7.68% year-over-year and its low cost of living percentile rank of 33, indicating a relatively expensive city. The combination of these metrics suggests a challenging environment for businesses to balance labor costs and talent attraction.

Labor Demand

The employment growth rate in Miami-Fort Lauderdale-West Palm Beach is 0.35% year-over-year, accompanied by a 0.285% deviation in weekly hours above the city's own trend. This combination signals a genuine demand expansion, albeit a modest one, as both jobs and hours are increasing. The labor demand composite score of 5.08 ranks near the median at the 40th percentile, indicating a stable but not exceptional labor market.

Unemployment

The unemployment rate in Miami-Fort Lauderdale-West Palm Beach is 3.70%, ranking at the 66th percentile, which indicates a relatively tight labor market. This tightness implies that businesses may face challenges in hiring, as the pool of available workers is smaller, and may need to offer competitive wages to attract talent. The practical implication is that companies may experience more wage pressure in this market.

Wage Growth

The year-over-year wage growth rate in Miami-Fort Lauderdale-West Palm Beach is 7.68%, ranking at the 96th percentile, which is exceptionally high. This rapid wage growth signals rising labor costs for employers but also indicates strong worker purchasing power. Businesses should be prepared for increasing labor expenses, but the high wages can also attract and retain top talent.

Cost of Living

Miami-Fort Lauderdale-West Palm Beach has a cost of living percentile rank of 33, indicating that it is relatively expensive compared to its peers, with a PSF to earnings ratio of $355/sqft to $35.24/hr, or 10.07. Although the PSF is falling by 0.8% year-over-year, the city remains less affordable. This means that businesses may need to offer wage premiums to attract talent without the natural advantage of a low cost of living.

Labor Force Growth

The civilian labor force in Miami-Fort Lauderdale-West Palm Beach is growing at a rate of 0.39% year-over-year, ranking at the 76th percentile. This positive growth rate indicates that the workforce supply is expanding, which is a favorable condition for businesses looking to hire. The implication is that companies have a growing pool of potential employees to draw from.

Building Permits

The year-over-year change in residential building permits in Miami-Fort Lauderdale-West Palm Beach is -34.10%, ranking at the 4th percentile, which is significantly low. This sharp decline in building permits signals a tightening housing supply, which could lead to future affordability issues and challenges in accommodating a growing workforce. Businesses should consider the potential long-term implications of a constrained housing market on their ability to attract and retain employees.

Days on Market

The current median days on market for homes in Miami-Fort Lauderdale-West Palm Beach is 85 days, with a year-over-year decrease of 4.5%. This indicates a relatively fast-moving market, which can be challenging for relocating workers. The low days on market suggest a competitive housing environment, where homes sell quickly, potentially making it difficult for employees to find and secure housing.

Office Economy

Miami-Fort Lauderdale-West Palm Beach has an office economy percentile rank of 64, indicating a deep professional talent pool. This makes the city well-suited for businesses in tech, finance, consulting, and HQ decisions that require specialized knowledge workers. However, it may be less ideal for industries that are more industrial or logistics-dominant.

In conclusion, Miami-Fort Lauderdale-West Palm Beach offers businesses a unique combination of high wage growth and a growing labor force, but it also presents challenges such as a tight labor market and a relatively expensive cost of living. The single biggest risk or constraint for decision-makers is the sharply declining building permits, which could exacerbate affordability issues and hinder the city's ability to accommodate a growing workforce, ultimately affecting businesses' ability to attract and retain talent.
