Economic Analysis

The Virginia Beach-Chesapeake-Norfolk metro area has an overall grade of C- with a composite score ranking it 35.6th percentile out of 50 US metros. This city's economic character is most defined by its below-average labor demand, with a composite score of 3.98, and its above-average wage growth of 4.48% year-over-year. The labor demand and wage growth metrics suggest a complex economic environment, with a mix of job market contraction and rising labor costs.

Labor Demand

The employment growth rate in this metro area is -0.23% year-over-year, and weekly hours are deviating -0.074% from the city's own 12-month baseline. This combination signals a contraction in labor demand, indicating that the job market is not expanding. The negative employment growth rate and below-trend hours suggest that businesses may not be adding new jobs at a significant pace.

Unemployment

The unemployment rate in this metro area is 3.80%, ranking near the median at the 42nd percentile. This suggests that the labor market has some slack, making it slightly easier for businesses to hire compared to tighter markets. However, the relatively low unemployment rate still implies some competition for talent, and businesses may need to offer competitive wages to attract workers.

Wage Growth

The year-over-year wage growth in this metro area is 4.48%, which is above average, ranking at the 66th percentile. This rapid wage growth signals rising labor costs for employers, but it also indicates strong worker purchasing power. As wages continue to grow, businesses may need to adjust their pricing strategies or operational efficiencies to maintain profitability.

Cost of Living

The cost of living in this metro area is below average, with a percentile rank of 26th, due to a PSF of $222/sqft and average hourly earnings of $34.92, resulting in a ratio of 6.36. However, the PSF is increasing by 2.3% year-over-year, which may erode affordability over time. The relatively low affordability score suggests that businesses may need to offer wage premiums to attract talent, as the cost of living is relatively high compared to other metros.

Labor Force Growth

The civilian labor force in this metro area is contracting at a rate of -1.37% year-over-year, ranking in the bottom tier at the 18th percentile. This decline in labor force supply implies a structural headwind for hiring, as the pool of available workers is shrinking. Businesses may need to invest in workforce development or recruitment strategies to attract talent from other areas.

Building Permits

The number of residential building permits in this metro area is decreasing by 17.54% year-over-year, ranking in the bottom tier at the 18th percentile. This sharp decline in building permits suggests a tightening housing supply, which may lead to affordability challenges and difficulties in accommodating a growing workforce. As housing supply contracts, businesses may need to consider the potential impact on employee relocation and retention.

Days on Market

The median days on market for homes in this metro area is 45 days, with a year-over-year increase of 2.3%. This relatively slow market, ranking near the median at the 56th percentile, suggests that workers relocating to this city may have a more accessible and less competitive housing market. However, the rising days on market may also indicate a normalization of the market, rather than a demand erosion.

Office Economy

The professional and office worker share in this metro area is below average, ranking at the 24th percentile. This suggests a relatively shallow talent pool for businesses requiring specialized knowledge-economy workers, such as tech, finance, or consulting firms. While this city may be suitable for industrial or logistics-dominant businesses, it may not be the best fit for companies requiring a deep pool of professional talent.

The Virginia Beach-Chesapeake-Norfolk metro area offers a complex economic environment with both opportunities and challenges for businesses. While the above-average wage growth and relatively low unemployment rate suggest a strong labor market, the below-average labor demand and declining labor force supply pose significant risks for hiring and workforce development. The single biggest risk or constraint for decision-makers is the potential affordability challenges and tightening housing supply, which may impact employee relocation and retention.
