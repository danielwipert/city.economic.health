Economic Analysis

The Atlanta-Sandy Springs-Roswell metro area has an overall grade of B- with a composite score ranking it at the 49.9th percentile out of 50 US metros. This city's economic character is most defined by its low labor demand, with a composite score in the bottom tier, and its high labor force growth rate, at 1.93% year-over-year. The labor demand metric, in particular, signals a contraction in the job market, with employment growth at -0.11% year-over-year.

Labor Demand

The employment growth rate in Atlanta is -0.11% year-over-year, and weekly hours are deviating from the trend by +0.454%. This combination signals a contraction in the job market, rather than genuine demand expansion, as hours are not increasing in line with job growth. The labor demand composite score of 3.69 is in the bottom tier, indicating a weak job market.

Unemployment

The unemployment rate in Atlanta is 3.50%, which is relatively low and corresponds to an above-average percentile rank of 72. This indicates a tight labor market with some slack, making it moderately challenging for businesses to hire. The low unemployment rate implies that businesses may face wage pressure and competition for talent.

Wage Growth

The year-over-year wage growth rate in Atlanta is +2.99%, which is near the median. This moderate wage growth rate suggests that labor costs for employers are increasing, but not excessively, and worker purchasing power is relatively stable. The wage growth rate is not exceptionally high, which may limit the attractiveness of the city for businesses seeking to minimize labor costs.

Cost of Living

Atlanta has a cost of living percentile rank of 63, indicating that it is more affordable than many of its peer cities. The ratio of $197 per square foot to $37.03 per hour is relatively high, but the city's affordability is supported by a stable year-over-year change in PSF. This makes Atlanta an attractive location for talent, as businesses may not need to offer significant wage premiums to compensate for high living costs.

Labor Force Growth

The civilian labor force in Atlanta is growing at a rate of 1.93% year-over-year, which is in the top tier. This expansion in labor supply suggests that the city has a growing workforce, making it easier for businesses to hire and expand their operations. The increasing labor force growth rate is a positive indicator for businesses looking to establish or grow their presence in the city.

Building Permits

The number of residential building permits in Atlanta is increasing by 7.37% year-over-year, which is an above-average rate. This expansion in housing supply suggests that the city is likely to experience improving affordability and a more accommodating environment for its growing workforce. The increase in building permits is a positive signal for businesses concerned about the long-term affordability and livability of the city.

Days on Market

The median days on market for homes in Atlanta is currently 56 days, with a year-over-year increase of 3.7%. This indicates a relatively stable and accessible housing market for workers relocating to the city. The moderate days on market suggest that the city's housing market is not excessively competitive, making it easier for businesses to attract and retain talent.

Office Economy

Atlanta has an office economy percentile rank of 42, indicating a moderately deep professional talent pool. The city is relatively well-suited for businesses in the tech, finance, and consulting sectors, but may not offer the same level of specialization as other cities with a more prominent office economy. The city's office economy is not exceptionally strong, which may limit its attractiveness for businesses seeking a highly specialized workforce.

In conclusion, Atlanta offers businesses a relatively affordable and growing labor market, with a moderate cost of living and expanding housing supply. However, the city's low labor demand and relatively weak office economy may pose a risk for businesses seeking to establish a strong presence in the city, particularly those in highly specialized industries. The single biggest risk or constraint for decision-makers is the city's weak job market, which may limit the availability of skilled talent and hinder business growth.
