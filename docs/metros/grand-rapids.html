Economic Analysis

The Grand Rapids-Wyoming-Kentwood metro area has an overall grade of C-, ranking 32.1th percentile out of 50 US metros, with a composite score driven largely by its low labor demand and stagnant wage growth. The labor demand composite score of 4.59, combining a +0.68% employment growth rate and a -0.658% weekly hours deviation, signals a contraction in genuine demand expansion. This, coupled with the bottom-tier wage growth, defines the city's current economic character.

Labor Demand

The employment growth rate of +0.68% and weekly hours deviation of -0.658% indicate a lack of genuine demand expansion, suggesting that while there is some job growth, it is not being accompanied by increased hours worked, which could signal a survivor squeeze where remaining workers absorb the load of eliminated roles. This combination does not bode well for businesses looking to expand or relocate, as it may indicate a lack of momentum in the labor market. The labor demand composite score of 4.59 further reinforces this notion, ranking below average at the 30th percentile.

Unemployment

The unemployment rate of 4.30% is relatively low, ranking above average at the 68th percentile, indicating a tight labor market with some slack. For businesses trying to hire in this market, the practical implication is that it may be harder to find and retain talent, potentially leading to upward pressure on wages. However, the relatively low unemployment rate also suggests that there is still some availability of labor, albeit with potential competition for skilled workers.

Wage Growth

The year-over-year wage growth rate of +1.41% is stagnant, ranking in the bottom tier at the 16th percentile, indicating slow labor cost increases for employers but weak bargaining power for workers. This slow wage growth may attract businesses looking for a flat cost environment, but it also suggests that worker purchasing power is not increasing significantly, which could impact local consumer demand. As a result, businesses may need to consider offering competitive wages to attract and retain top talent.

Cost of Living

The city's cost of living, with a PSF of $217/sqft and an earnings ratio of $32.58/hr, resulting in a ratio of 6.66, ranks in the bottom tier at the 10th percentile, indicating that the city is relatively expensive. This means that the city may struggle to attract talent without offering wage premiums, as the high cost of living could offset any potential benefits of relocating to the area. The 2.8% year-over-year increase in PSF further exacerbates this issue, making the city less competitive in terms of affordability.

Labor Force Growth

The year-over-year growth rate of the civilian labor force is -7.49%, ranking in the bottom tier at the 0th percentile, indicating a significant contraction in the labor force supply. This implies that the hiring capacity for businesses is structurally constrained, making it challenging to find and retain workers, especially in a tight labor market. The shrinking labor pool poses a significant risk for businesses looking to expand or relocate to the area.

Building Permits

The year-over-year change in building permits is -5.72%, ranking below average at the 34th percentile, indicating a tightening of the housing supply. This signals that future affordability and workforce accommodation may be at risk, as the lack of new housing developments could exacerbate the existing affordability issues and make it harder for workers to relocate to the area. The decline in building permits suggests that the city may struggle to provide adequate housing for its workforce, further constraining labor market growth.

Days on Market

The current median days on market is 38 days, with a year-over-year increase of 2.7%, ranking above average at the 70th percentile, indicating a slower market. For workers relocating to this city, the slower market means that they may have more time to find a suitable home, making the city more accessible in terms of housing. However, the rising days on market could also signal a normalization of the market, potentially indicating a shift away from a seller's market.

Office Economy

The share of jobs in professional and office sectors is relatively low, ranking in the bottom tier at the 16th percentile, indicating a shallow talent pool for businesses requiring specialized office workers. This city is best suited for businesses that do not require a deep knowledge-economy talent pool, such as industrial or logistics-dominant economies, but may not be ideal for tech, finance, or consulting firms that rely on a highly skilled workforce.

The Grand Rapids-Wyoming-Kentwood metro area offers businesses a relatively tight labor market with slow wage growth, but the significant contraction in the labor force supply and high cost of living pose significant risks for businesses looking to expand or relocate. The single biggest constraint for decision-makers is the shrinking labor pool, which could limit the city's ability to support business growth and expansion, making it essential for businesses to carefully consider these factors when evaluating the area as a potential location.
