Economic Analysis

The Denver-Aurora-Centennial metro area has an overall grade of C-, with a score of 34.7 out of 100, indicating significant challenges in its economy. One of the most defining aspects of this city's economy is its slow employment growth, with a mere 0.2% increase in jobs over the past year, and a relatively high unemployment rate of 4.3%, which is higher than most metros.

Labor Demand

Whether employers are adding jobs, based on job growth over the past year and changes in weekly work hours.

The city is adding jobs, albeit slowly, with a 0.2% increase over the past year, and workers are putting in more hours, with a 1.2% rise in weekly hours, indicating a moderate level of labor demand. This means that the job market is growing, but not at a pace that is significantly stronger than most other metros, with a score of 64 out of 100.

Unemployment

The share of people who want a job but can't find one; lower is better.

The unemployment rate of 4.3% makes it relatively harder for employers to hire, as it indicates a tighter labor market, but with a score of 14 out of 100, it is actually a weakness, as it is higher than most metros. This higher unemployment rate means that there are more people looking for work, but it also suggests that the local economy may not be as strong as others.

Wage Growth

How much average hourly pay rose over the past year.

Pay rose by 1.9% over the past year, which is slower than most metros, with a score of 22 out of 100, indicating that employers may not face significant pressure to increase wages, but workers may not see substantial gains in their paychecks. This slow wage growth means that the cost of labor is not rising quickly, but it also may not be enough to keep up with the cost of living.

Cost of Living

How many hours a local worker needs to work to pay for one square foot of a home, and whether that's improving.

The cost of living in Denver is about average, with a price per square foot of $277 and hourly pay of $43.06, and a score of 57 out of 100, indicating that housing is relatively affordable compared to other metros, and it is getting more affordable as prices per square foot have fallen by 3.5% over the past year. This affordability is a positive factor for attracting and retaining workers, as they can afford to live in the area without excessive financial strain.

Labor Force Growth

Whether the number of people working or looking for work is growing.

The pool of workers is shrinking, with a decline of 2.4% over the past year, and a score of 4 out of 100, indicating a significant weakness, as it will be harder for employers to find new workers and may lead to labor shortages. This decline in the labor force means that the city's economy may face structural challenges in terms of finding and retaining workers.

Building Permits

How many new homes builders got approval for, compared with a year ago.

New home approvals have fallen by 18.6% over the past year, with a score of 16 out of 100, indicating a weakness, as it suggests that the future housing supply may not keep up with demand, potentially leading to higher prices and reduced affordability. This decline in building permits is a concern, as it may exacerbate the city's housing affordability challenges and make it harder for workers to find homes.

Days on Market

How long a typical home for sale takes to find a buyer; about 35 to 80 days is healthiest.

Homes are taking 60 days to sell, which is getting slower, with a decline of 4.8% over the past year, and a score of 30 out of 100, indicating a weakness, as it suggests a softening housing market, where buyers may be pulling back. This slower pace of home sales is a concern, as it may indicate a weakening demand for housing and potentially affect the local economy.

Office Economy

The share of jobs in office-based fields like tech, finance and professional services.

The city has a large office-based workforce, with a score of 74 out of 100, indicating a strength, as it is larger than most metros, making it an attractive location for businesses in the tech, finance, and professional services sectors. This strong office economy is a positive factor, as it provides a deep talent pool and a suitable environment for companies that require specialized knowledge workers.

The bottom line is that Denver's biggest strength is its large office-based workforce, which makes it an attractive location for businesses in the tech, finance, and professional services sectors. However, its biggest risk is its slow labor force growth, which may lead to labor shortages and structural challenges for the local economy, making it harder for employers to find and retain workers.
