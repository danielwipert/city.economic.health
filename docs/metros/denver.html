Economic Analysis

The Denver-Aurora-Centennial metro area has an overall grade of C+ with a composite score ranking 43.5th percentile out of 50 US metros. This city's economic character is most defined by its above-average labor demand, with a composite score of 5.42, and its relatively low wage growth, at +1.94% year-over-year. The labor demand and wage growth metrics suggest a city with expanding job opportunities but moderate pressure on labor costs.

Labor Demand

The Denver-Aurora-Centennial metro area has an employment growth rate of +0.31% year-over-year and a weekly hours deviation of +0.687% from its own trend, indicating genuine demand expansion. This combination signals that the city is experiencing a real increase in labor demand, with both more jobs being added and hours worked above the trend. This is a positive sign for businesses looking to expand or establish operations in the area.

Unemployment

The unemployment rate in Denver-Aurora-Centennial is 4.10%, ranking near the median at the 48th percentile. This suggests that the labor market has some slack, making it slightly easier for businesses to hire compared to tighter markets. However, the relatively low unemployment rate still implies some competition for talent, and businesses may need to offer competitive wages to attract the best candidates.

Wage Growth

The year-over-year wage growth in Denver-Aurora-Centennial is +1.94%, which is below average, ranking at the 26th percentile. This moderate wage growth rate implies that labor costs for employers are rising, but at a slower pace than in many other cities. While this may be beneficial for businesses looking to control costs, it also means that workers may have less bargaining power and lower purchasing power.

Cost of Living

Denver-Aurora-Centennial has a cost of living score ranking at the 65th percentile, with a price-to-salary ratio of $280/sqft to $42.59/hr, or 6.57. This indicates that the city is relatively affordable compared to its peers, with a falling PSF value (-3.5% year-over-year) contributing to its high affordability score. This makes it an attractive location for businesses looking to recruit talent without having to offer significant wage premiums to offset high living costs.

Labor Force Growth

The civilian labor force in Denver-Aurora-Centennial is shrinking at a rate of -1.67% year-over-year, ranking at the 18th percentile. This contraction in the labor force supply implies that businesses may face structural headwinds when trying to hire, as the pool of available workers is decreasing. This could lead to increased competition for talent and upward pressure on wages.

Building Permits

The number of residential building permits in Denver-Aurora-Centennial has decreased by -17.89% year-over-year, ranking at the 20th percentile. This sharp decline in building permits suggests that the housing supply is tightening, which could lead to future affordability issues and challenges in accommodating a growing workforce. This is a concern for businesses that rely on a steady supply of skilled workers.

Days on Market

The median days on market for homes in Denver-Aurora-Centennial is 57 days, with a year-over-year decrease of -1.7%. This relatively fast pace of home sales, ranking at the 22nd percentile, indicates a competitive market that may be challenging for relocating workers to find housing. Businesses may need to consider offering relocation assistance or other incentives to attract talent to the area.

Office Economy

Denver-Aurora-Centennial has a deep professional talent pool, with an office economy score ranking at the 60th percentile. This suggests that the city is well-suited for businesses in the tech, finance, consulting, and HQ sectors, which require a high concentration of specialized knowledge workers. However, it may be less suitable for industries that rely on industrial or logistics workers.

The Denver-Aurora-Centennial metro area offers businesses a unique combination of above-average labor demand and relatively affordable living costs, making it an attractive location for talent recruitment. However, the single biggest risk or constraint for decision-makers is the shrinking labor force supply, which could lead to increased competition for talent and upward pressure on wages, potentially offsetting the benefits of the city's affordability and labor demand.
