Economic Analysis

The Denver-Aurora-Centennial metro area has an overall grade of C- with a composite score ranking it 36.5th percentile out of 50 US metros. The city's economic character is most defined by its labor demand, with a composite score of 5.94 ranking it in the 68th percentile, and its high unemployment rate of 4.60%, which ranks it in the 12th percentile. These metrics suggest a mixed economic environment, with some indicators pointing to growth and others to challenges.

Labor Demand

The Denver-Aurora-Centennial metro area has an employment growth rate of +0.10% and a weekly hours deviation of +1.564% from its own trend, signaling genuine demand expansion. This combination indicates that the city is adding jobs and workers are putting in more hours, suggesting a strong labor market. The labor demand composite score of 5.94, ranking in the 68th percentile, further supports this assessment.

Unemployment

The unemployment rate in Denver-Aurora-Centennial is 4.60%, ranking it in the 12th percentile, indicating a relatively high level of unemployment. This suggests that the labor market has some slack, making it easier for businesses to hire workers. However, this also implies weaker local consumer demand, which could impact businesses that rely on local spending.

Wage Growth

The year-over-year wage growth in Denver-Aurora-Centennial is +2.15%, ranking it in the 28th percentile, indicating moderate wage growth. This rate of wage growth suggests that labor costs for employers are rising, but at a slower pace than in many other cities. Workers in the area will see some increase in their purchasing power, but it may not be as strong as in other metros.

Cost of Living

Denver-Aurora-Centennial has a cost of living score ranking it in the 61st percentile, with a PSF of $279/sqft and average hourly earnings of $43.51/hr, resulting in a ratio of 6.41. This suggests that the city is relatively affordable compared to its peers, with a falling PSF (-3.1% YoY) contributing to its high affordability score. This makes it an attractive location for talent, as businesses may not need to offer wage premiums to compensate for high living costs.

Labor Force Growth

The civilian labor force in Denver-Aurora-Centennial is shrinking at a rate of -2.99% year-over-year, ranking it in the 6th percentile. This contraction in labor force supply poses a significant challenge for businesses looking to hire, as it may lead to a shortage of skilled workers. The implications for hiring capacity are negative, making it harder for companies to find the talent they need.

Building Permits

The number of building permits in Denver-Aurora-Centennial has decreased by -19.62% year-over-year, ranking it in the 16th percentile. This sharp decline in permits suggests that housing supply is tightening, which could lead to future affordability issues and make it harder for workers to relocate to the area. This is a concern for businesses that rely on a steady influx of new talent.

Days on Market

The median days on market for homes in Denver-Aurora-Centennial is 58 days, with a year-over-year decrease of -3.3%. This relatively fast pace of home sales, ranking in the 29th percentile, indicates a competitive market that may be challenging for relocating workers to find housing. However, this could also suggest a strong demand for housing, which could be beneficial for businesses looking to attract talent.

Office Economy

Denver-Aurora-Centennial has a professional and office worker share of 2.92, ranking it in the 64th percentile. This suggests a deep talent pool suited for tech, finance, consulting, and HQ decisions, making it an attractive location for businesses in these sectors. However, it may be less suitable for industries that require a large workforce in industrial or logistics roles.

The Denver-Aurora-Centennial metro area offers a mixed economic environment, with some indicators pointing to growth and others to challenges. While the city has a relatively affordable cost of living and a deep professional talent pool, its high unemployment rate, shrinking labor force, and tightening housing supply pose significant risks for businesses looking to locate there. The single biggest constraint for decision-makers is the shrinking labor force, which could lead to a shortage of skilled workers and make it harder for companies to find the talent they need.
