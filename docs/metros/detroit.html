Economic Analysis

The Detroit-Warren-Dearborn metro area has an overall grade of D, ranking in the 19.0th percentile out of 50 US metros, with a labor demand composite score of 3.58, placing it in the bottom tier. The city's economic character is most defined by its low labor demand and high unemployment rate, with employment growth at -0.20% year-over-year and an unemployment rate of 5.70%. These metrics signal significant challenges for businesses looking to expand or relocate in the area.

Labor Demand

The employment growth rate in Detroit-Warren-Dearborn is -0.20% year-over-year, and weekly hours are deviating from the trend by +0.439%. This combination signals a survivor squeeze, where remaining workers are absorbing the load of eliminated roles, rather than genuine demand expansion. The labor demand composite score of 3.58 is in the bottom tier, indicating a weak job market.

Unemployment

The unemployment rate in Detroit-Warren-Dearborn is 5.70%, placing it in the bottom tier. This signals a market with significant slack, making it easier for businesses to hire workers. However, the high unemployment rate also implies weaker local consumer demand, which could impact businesses reliant on local spending.

Wage Growth

The year-over-year wage growth rate in Detroit-Warren-Dearborn is +1.48%, which is relatively slow. This moderate wage growth implies a relatively flat labor cost environment for employers, but also means workers have limited bargaining power. As a result, businesses may not face significant pressure to increase wages, but workers may not have strong purchasing power.

Cost of Living

Detroit-Warren-Dearborn has a cost of living score in the 26th percentile, with a price-to-salary ratio of $186/sqft to $37.53/hr, indicating a relatively affordable city. The fact that the PSF is rising 3.3% year-over-year, however, may erode this affordability advantage over time. The current affordability advantage could attract talent without requiring significant wage premiums.

Labor Force Growth

The civilian labor force in Detroit-Warren-Dearborn is contracting at a rate of -1.76% year-over-year. This decline in labor force supply implies a structural headwind for hiring, making it challenging for businesses to find and retain workers. As the labor force shrinks, businesses may need to offer more competitive wages or benefits to attract talent.

Building Permits

The number of residential building permits in Detroit-Warren-Dearborn has decreased by -32.95% year-over-year. This sharp decline signals a tightening housing supply, which could lead to decreased affordability and make it harder for workers to relocate to the area. The reduced housing supply may constrain workforce growth and limit the city's ability to attract new businesses.

Days on Market

The median days on market for homes in Detroit-Warren-Dearborn is 43 days, with a year-over-year increase of +7.5%. This slower market, ranked in the 80th percentile, makes it more accessible for workers relocating to the city, as they have more time to find a home. The rising days on market may also indicate a normalization of the housing market, rather than a demand-driven slowdown.

Office Economy

The professional and office worker share in Detroit-Warren-Dearborn is ranked in the 38th percentile, indicating a relatively shallow talent pool. This city is less suited for businesses requiring a deep knowledge-economy talent pool, such as tech or finance companies, but may be more suitable for industrial or logistics-dominant businesses.

The Detroit-Warren-Dearborn metro area offers businesses an affordable cost of living and a slower housing market, making it more accessible for relocating workers. However, the single biggest risk or constraint for decision-makers is the city's weak labor demand and high unemployment rate, which may limit the availability of skilled workers and constrain business growth.
