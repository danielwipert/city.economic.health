Economic Analysis

The Detroit-Warren-Dearborn metro area has an overall grade of D, ranking in the 19.0th percentile out of 50 US metros, with a labor demand composite score of 3.58. This city's economic character is most defined by its low unemployment rate of 5.70% and its negative employment growth rate of -0.20%. The combination of these metrics signals a challenging labor market for businesses.

Labor Demand

The employment growth rate in Detroit-Warren-Dearborn is -0.20%, indicating a contraction in job opportunities, while the weekly hours deviation from trend is +0.439%, suggesting that remaining workers are absorbing the load of eliminated roles, characteristic of a survivor squeeze. This combination signals that the labor market is not experiencing genuine demand expansion. The labor demand composite score of 3.58 falls in the bottom tier, at the 16th percentile.

Unemployment

The unemployment rate in Detroit-Warren-Dearborn is 5.70%, which is relatively high, indicating that the labor market has some slack. This means that businesses may find it easier to hire workers, but the local consumer demand may be weaker. The unemployment rate ranks in the bottom tier, at the 2nd percentile, suggesting significant challenges in the labor market.

Wage Growth

The year-over-year wage growth in Detroit-Warren-Dearborn is +1.48%, which is relatively slow. This slow wage growth implies that labor costs for employers are not rising rapidly, but worker purchasing power is also not increasing significantly. The wage growth rate ranks in the bottom tier, at the 18th percentile.

Cost of Living

Detroit-Warren-Dearborn has a cost of living score that ranks in the 26th percentile, with a PSF of $186/sqft and average hourly earnings of $37.53/hr, resulting in a ratio of 4.96. This indicates that the city is relatively expensive compared to its peers. The cost of living is not a significant advantage for talent attraction without wage premiums.

Labor Force Growth

The civilian labor force in Detroit-Warren-Dearborn is contracting at a rate of -1.76% year-over-year, indicating that the workforce supply is shrinking. This contraction implies that businesses may face structural headwinds in hiring and expanding their operations. The labor force growth rate ranks below average, at the 30th percentile.

Building Permits

The year-over-year change in residential building permits in Detroit-Warren-Dearborn is -32.95%, indicating a sharp decline in housing supply expansion. This decline signals that future affordability and workforce accommodation may be at risk, potentially leading to a supply squeeze.

Days on Market

The median days on market for homes in Detroit-Warren-Dearborn is 43 days, with a year-over-year increase of +7.5%. This increase indicates a slower market, ranking in the 80th percentile, which may be more accessible for workers relocating to the city.

Office Economy

The share of professional and office workers in Detroit-Warren-Dearborn is relatively low, ranking in the 38th percentile. This suggests that the city has a less deep talent pool for businesses in the tech, finance, or consulting sectors, making it less suited for HQ decisions or knowledge-economy driven businesses.

The Detroit-Warren-Dearborn metro area offers businesses a labor market with some slack, potentially making it easier to hire workers, but the slow wage growth and declining labor force supply are significant concerns. The single biggest risk or constraint for decision-makers is the city's low labor demand and shrinking workforce, which may limit business expansion and growth opportunities.
