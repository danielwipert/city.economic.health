Economic Analysis

The Detroit-Warren-Dearborn metro area has an overall grade of C- with a composite score ranking it 27.4th percentile out of 50 US metros. The city's economic character is most defined by its low unemployment rate of 6.00% and its stagnant wage growth of +0.60% year-over-year, which together signal a challenging labor market. Specifically, the labor demand composite score of 4.43 and the unemployment rate are the two metrics that most define this city's current economic situation.

Labor Demand

The employment growth rate is +0.16% year-over-year, and weekly hours are deviating -0.098% from the city's own 12-month baseline, indicating a labor demand composite score that is below average at the 30th percentile. This combination signals a contraction in labor demand, rather than genuine demand expansion or survivor squeeze. The below-average labor demand suggests that the city is not adding jobs at a rapid pace.

Unemployment

The actual unemployment rate is 6.00%, which is in the bottom tier at the 2nd percentile, indicating a tight labor market with significant slack. This means that businesses trying to hire in this city may face challenges in finding available workers, but the slack in the market could also mean less wage pressure. However, the low unemployment rate relative to the national average suggests that the labor market is more competitive than it seems.

Wage Growth

The year-over-year wage growth rate is +0.60%, which is in the bottom tier at the 12th percentile, indicating stagnant wage growth. This slow wage growth means that employer labor costs are not rising rapidly, but it also implies weak bargaining power for workers and potentially limited purchasing power. As a result, businesses may not face significant labor cost pressures, but workers may not have the disposable income to drive local consumer demand.

Cost of Living

The city has a cost of living score that is below average at the 26th percentile, with a PSF of $186/sqft and average hourly earnings of $36.68/hr, resulting in a ratio of 5.07. This means that the city is relatively expensive compared to its peers, which could make it challenging to attract talent without offering wage premiums. The fact that PSF is rising +1.6% year-over-year relative to wages further exacerbates the affordability concerns.

Labor Force Growth

The civilian labor force is growing at a rate of +0.69% year-over-year, which is above average at the 74th percentile. This indicates that the labor force supply is expanding, which could provide a hiring capacity advantage for businesses locating in the city. The growing labor force suggests that the city has a relatively healthy demographic trend.

Building Permits

The year-over-year change in building permits is -18.71%, which is in the bottom tier at the 18th percentile. This decline in permits suggests that housing supply is tightening, which could lead to future affordability concerns and challenges in accommodating a growing workforce. The sharp decline in building permits is a red flag for businesses that rely on a dynamic and growing labor market.

Days on Market

The current median days on market is 40 days, with a year-over-year increase of +5.3%, which puts it in the top tier at the 82nd percentile. This means that homes are sitting on the market for a relatively long time, indicating a slower and more buyer-friendly market. For workers relocating to this city, this could make it easier to find a home, but it also suggests that the local real estate market may be normalizing after a period of rapid growth.

Office Economy

The city has an office economy score that is near the median at the 40th percentile, with a share of professional and office workers of 2.22 out of 5. This indicates a moderately deep talent pool, making it suited for businesses that require specialized office skills, but not necessarily a hub for tech, finance, or consulting companies. The city's office economy is neither a major strength nor a weakness, but rather a neutral factor in the location decision.

The Detroit-Warren-Dearborn metro area offers businesses a unique combination of a tight labor market and stagnant wage growth, which could be both a challenge and an opportunity. However, the single biggest risk or constraint for businesses locating in this city is the tightening housing supply and rising cost of living, which could lead to future affordability concerns and challenges in attracting and retaining talent.
