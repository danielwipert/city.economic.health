Economic Analysis

The Detroit-Warren-Dearborn metro area has an overall grade of C- with a composite score ranking it 29.5th percentile out of 50 US metros. The city's economic character is most defined by its low unemployment rate of 5.40% and its sluggish labor demand composite score of 4.43, which ranks in the 30th percentile. These metrics suggest a challenging hiring environment with limited job growth.

Labor Demand

The employment growth rate in Detroit-Warren-Dearborn is a modest +0.16% year-over-year, while weekly hours are running -0.098% below trend. This combination signals a lack of genuine demand expansion, as hours are not increasing despite some job growth, indicating a potential survivor squeeze where remaining workers are absorbing the load of eliminated roles.

Unemployment

The unemployment rate in Detroit-Warren-Dearborn is 5.40%, ranking in the 2nd percentile, indicating a very tight labor market with significant slack. For a business trying to hire in this city, the low unemployment rate means it will be harder to find and recruit talent, and may lead to upward pressure on wages.

Wage Growth

The year-over-year wage growth in Detroit-Warren-Dearborn is +0.60%, ranking in the 12th percentile, indicating stagnant wage growth. This slow wage growth means labor costs for employers will remain relatively flat, but workers will have weak bargaining power and limited purchasing power.

Cost of Living

Detroit-Warren-Dearborn has a cost of living score ranking it in the 31st percentile, with a PSF of $186/sqft and average hourly earnings of $36.68/hr, resulting in a ratio of 5.07. This suggests the city is relatively expensive compared to its peers. The modest affordability means businesses may need to offer wage premiums to attract talent, as the city's cost of living is not a significant draw.

Labor Force Growth

The civilian labor force in Detroit-Warren-Dearborn is growing at a rate of +2.68% year-over-year, ranking in the 94th percentile. This rapid expansion of the labor force means the supply of workers is increasing, which should ease hiring capacity and provide a larger talent pool for businesses to draw from.

Building Permits

The number of building permits in Detroit-Warren-Dearborn has decreased by -18.71% year-over-year, ranking in the 18th percentile. This decline in permits suggests that housing supply is tightening, which may lead to decreased affordability and increased competition for workforce accommodation in the future.

Days on Market

The median days on market in Detroit-Warren-Dearborn is currently 39 days, with a year-over-year increase of +2.6%, ranking in the 74th percentile. This indicates a relatively slow market, which should make it more accessible for relocating workers to find housing, as homes are sitting on the market for a longer period.

Office Economy

The share of professional and office workers in Detroit-Warren-Dearborn ranks in the 40th percentile, indicating a moderately deep talent pool. This city is best suited for businesses that can leverage its existing industrial and logistics expertise, but may not be the ideal location for tech, finance, or consulting firms that require a more specialized knowledge-economy talent pool.

The Detroit-Warren-Dearborn metro area offers businesses a unique combination of a tight labor market and a rapidly expanding labor force, but the single biggest risk or constraint is the potential for decreased affordability and increased competition for workforce accommodation due to the decline in building permits. Decision-makers should carefully weigh these factors when considering a location decision in this city.
