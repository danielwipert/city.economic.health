Economic Analysis

The Detroit-Warren-Dearborn metro area has an overall grade of D, with a score of 24.5 out of 100, indicating significant economic challenges. The city's economy is currently defined by a weak job market, with employment growth of -0.16% over the past year, and an unemployment rate of 5.60%, which is higher than most of the 50 metros.

Labor Demand

Whether employers are adding jobs, based on job growth over the past year and changes in weekly work hours.

The city is losing jobs, with a decline of 0.16% over the past year, and workers' hours are only slightly rising, by 0.4%, indicating a lack of strong demand for labor. This means that the job market is not robust, and employers may not be looking to hire as many new workers, with a score of 20, which is below average compared to other metros.

Unemployment

The share of people who want a job but can't find one; lower is better.

The unemployment rate of 5.60% makes it relatively easy for employers to hire, as there are more workers available, but this also indicates a weaker local economy, with a score of 2, which is in the bottom tier compared to other metros. This high unemployment rate means that employers may have an easier time finding workers, but it also suggests that the local consumer demand may be weaker.

Wage Growth

How much average hourly pay rose over the past year.

Wages rose by 2.10% over the past year, which is a relatively slow pace, meaning that employers' labor costs are not rising too quickly, but workers' paychecks are not growing rapidly either, with a score of 24, which is below average. This slow wage growth may help keep labor costs in check for employers, but it also means that workers may not have as much purchasing power.

Cost of Living

How many hours a local worker needs to work to pay for one square foot of a home, and whether that's improving.

The cost of living in Detroit is relatively high, with a price per square foot of $185 and hourly pay of $36.96, and it is getting worse, with housing prices rising by 3.4% over the past year, making it less affordable for local workers, with a score of 24, which is below average. This means that the city may struggle to attract and retain workers who are looking for a more affordable place to live.

Labor Force Growth

Whether the number of people working or looking for work is growing.

The pool of workers in Detroit is growing, but only slowly, at a rate of 0.49% over the past year, which is above average, but still may not be enough to meet the needs of employers, with a score of 66. This growth in the labor force is a positive sign, but it may not be enough to offset the city's other economic challenges.

Building Permits

How many new homes builders got approval for, compared with a year ago.

New home approvals in Detroit have declined sharply, by 35.82% over the past year, which signals that the future housing supply may be limited, and affordability may worsen, with a score of 4, which is in the bottom tier. This decline in building permits is a concern, as it may lead to a shortage of housing options and higher prices in the future.

Days on Market

How long a typical home for sale takes to find a buyer; about 35 to 80 days is healthiest.

Homes in Detroit are taking 45 days to sell, which is relatively fast, and this time is getting shorter, by 7.1% over the past year, indicating a relatively healthy housing market, with a score of 82, which is better than most of the 50 metros. This fast pace of home sales is a positive sign, but it may also indicate that the market is competitive, and buyers may need to act quickly.

Office Economy

The share of jobs in office-based fields like tech, finance and professional services.

Detroit's office-based workforce is near the median compared to other metros, with a score of 50, which means that the city has a moderate-sized pool of workers in tech, finance, and professional services. This makes the city a suitable location for businesses that need a mix of office and non-office workers, but it may not be the best fit for companies that require a large pool of specialized office workers.

The bottom line is that Detroit's biggest strength is its relatively fast-paced housing market, but its biggest risk is its weak job market and high unemployment rate, which may make it challenging for the city to attract and retain workers and businesses. Overall, the city's economic challenges, including slow wage growth and a high cost of living, make it a less competitive location for businesses and workers compared to other metros.
