Economic Analysis

The Detroit-Warren-Dearborn metro area has an overall grade of D, with a score of 24.5 out of 100, indicating significant economic challenges. One of the most defining features of this city's economy right now is its weak labor demand, with employment growth of -0.2% over the past year, which is a major concern for businesses looking to expand or relocate.

Labor Demand

Whether employers are adding jobs, based on job growth over the past year and changes in weekly work hours.

The city is losing jobs, with a decline of 0.2% over the past year, and while workers' hours are rising by 0.4%, this is still a weakness in the job market, indicating that the city is not adding jobs at a pace that can support economic growth. This slow job growth means that businesses may struggle to find the talent they need to expand or start new operations.

Unemployment

The share of people who want a job but can't find one; lower is better.

The unemployment rate in Detroit-Warren-Dearborn is 5.6%, which is higher than most metros, making it easier for employers to hire, but also indicating weaker local consumer demand. With a score of 2 out of 100, the city's unemployment rate is a significant weakness, suggesting that many workers are struggling to find employment.

Wage Growth

How much average hourly pay rose over the past year.

Pay rose by 2.1% over the past year, which is slower than most metros, meaning that employers may not face significant wage pressure, but workers may not see much improvement in their paychecks. This slow wage growth, with a score of 24 out of 100, is a weakness for the city, as it may make it harder to attract and retain top talent.

Cost of Living

How many hours a local worker needs to work to pay for one square foot of a home, and whether that's improving.

Housing in Detroit-Warren-Dearborn is less affordable than most metros, with a price per square foot of $185 and hourly pay of $36.96, and it's getting worse, with prices rising by 3.4% over the past year. This makes it harder for workers to afford housing, and with a score of 24 out of 100, the city's cost of living is a significant weakness.

Labor Force Growth

Whether the number of people working or looking for work is growing.

The pool of workers in Detroit-Warren-Dearborn is growing at a rate of 0.5% over the past year, which is faster than most metros, making it easier for businesses to find the talent they need. This labor force growth, with a score of 66 out of 100, is a strength for the city, as it provides a larger pool of potential employees for businesses to draw from.

Building Permits

How many new homes builders got approval for, compared with a year ago.

New home approvals in Detroit-Warren-Dearborn declined by 35.8% over the past year, which is a significant weakness, indicating that the city's housing supply may not be keeping up with demand. This decline in building permits, with a score of 4 out of 100, suggests that the city may face a housing shortage in the future, making it harder for workers to find affordable housing.

Days on Market

How long a typical home for sale takes to find a buyer; about 35 to 80 days is healthiest.

Homes in Detroit-Warren-Dearborn are taking 45 days to sell, which is getting slower, with a 7.1% increase over the past year, but this is still a relatively healthy housing market, with a score of 82 out of 100. This indicates that the city's housing market is balanced, with neither too much nor too little supply, making it easier for buyers and sellers to negotiate.

Office Economy

The share of jobs in office-based fields like tech, finance and professional services.

The city's office-based workforce, including tech, finance, and professional services, is about average compared to other metros, with a score of 50 out of 100, making it suitable for a range of businesses, but not particularly specialized in any one sector. This average office economy means that the city may not have a deep talent pool in specific areas, but it can still support a variety of businesses and industries.

The bottom line is that Detroit-Warren-Dearborn's biggest strength is its growing labor force, which provides a larger pool of potential employees for businesses to draw from, but its biggest risk is its weak labor demand, which may make it harder for businesses to expand or start new operations. Overall, the city's economic challenges, including high unemployment and slow wage growth, make it a less attractive location for businesses and workers alike.
