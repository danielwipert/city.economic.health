Economic Analysis

The Detroit-Warren-Dearborn metro area has an overall grade of C- with a composite score ranking it 30.5th percentile out of 50 US metros. The city's economic character is most defined by its low unemployment rate of 5.40% and its sluggish labor demand composite score of 4.43, which ranks in the 30th percentile. These metrics suggest a challenging environment for businesses looking to hire and expand.

Labor Demand

The employment growth rate in Detroit-Warren-Dearborn is a modest +0.16% year-over-year, while weekly hours are running -0.098% below trend. This combination signals a lack of genuine demand expansion, as hours are not increasing despite some job growth, which may indicate a survivor squeeze where remaining workers are absorbing the load of eliminated roles.

Unemployment

The unemployment rate in Detroit-Warren-Dearborn is 5.40%, ranking in the 2nd percentile, indicating a very tight labor market with significant slack. For a business trying to hire in this market, the low unemployment rate means it will be harder to find available workers, leading to potential wage pressure.

Wage Growth

The year-over-year wage growth in Detroit-Warren-Dearborn is +0.60%, ranking in the 12th percentile, indicating stagnant wage growth. This slow wage growth environment means labor costs for employers are not rising quickly, but it also signifies weak bargaining power for workers and potentially limited purchasing power.

Cost of Living

Detroit-Warren-Dearborn has a cost of living score ranking it in the 31st percentile, with a price-to-salary ratio of $186/sqft to $36.68/hr, or 5.07. This indicates the city is relatively expensive compared to its peers. The modest affordability, as signaled by the low percentile rank, may require businesses to offer wage premiums to attract talent.

Labor Force Growth

The civilian labor force in Detroit-Warren-Dearborn is growing at a rate of +2.68% year-over-year, ranking in the 94th percentile. This significant expansion in labor force supply is a positive sign for businesses looking to hire, as it indicates a growing pool of potential workers.

Building Permits

The number of building permits in Detroit-Warren-Dearborn has decreased by -10.99% year-over-year, ranking in the 28th percentile. This decline in residential building permits suggests that housing supply is tightening, which could lead to future affordability issues and challenges in accommodating a growing workforce.

Days on Market

Homes in Detroit-Warren-Dearborn are currently sitting on the market for a median of 39 days, with a year-over-year increase of +2.6%, ranking in the 74th percentile. This slower market, as indicated by the high percentile rank for days on market, means that workers relocating to this city may find a more accessible and less competitive housing market.

Office Economy

The share of professional and office workers in Detroit-Warren-Dearborn ranks in the 40th percentile, indicating a moderately deep talent pool. This city is best suited for businesses that can thrive in a mixed economy, but it may not be ideal for those requiring a highly specialized or deep knowledge-economy talent pool, such as tech or finance hubs.

In conclusion, Detroit-Warren-Dearborn offers businesses a unique combination of a tight labor market and modestly expensive cost of living, alongside a rapidly growing labor force. However, the single biggest risk or constraint for decision-makers is the potential for future affordability issues and workforce accommodation challenges due to the decline in building permits and tightening housing supply.
