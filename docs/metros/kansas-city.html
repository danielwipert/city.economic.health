Economic Analysis

Kansas City earns an overall grade of B+ with a composite score ranking it at the 61.4th percentile among 50 US metros. The city's economic character is most defined by its strong wage growth, with a year-over-year increase of 7.35%, and its above-average labor demand, driven by employment growth of 0.73% and hours worked 0.665% above trend. These metrics suggest a city with a robust job market and rising labor costs.

Labor Demand

Kansas City's labor demand is characterized by employment growth of 0.73% and weekly hours 0.665% above its own 12-month trend, resulting in a composite score of 5.99, which ranks at the 70th percentile. This combination signals genuine demand expansion, as both jobs and hours worked are increasing. The city's labor market is experiencing a period of growth, with employers adding jobs and workers putting in more hours.

Unemployment

The unemployment rate in Kansas City is 3.90%, ranking at the 64th percentile, indicating a relatively tight labor market. With a low unemployment rate, businesses may face challenges in hiring, as the pool of available workers is smaller, and may need to offer competitive wages to attract talent. This tight market can lead to upward pressure on wages, making it essential for businesses to consider this factor in their hiring strategies.

Wage Growth

Wages in Kansas City are growing at a rate of 7.35% year-over-year, placing the city at the 92nd percentile. This rapid wage growth indicates a strong labor market, where workers have significant bargaining power, and employers may face rising labor costs. As a result, workers in Kansas City have increasing purchasing power, which can benefit local businesses that rely on consumer spending.

Cost of Living

Kansas City has a cost of living score that ranks at the 79th percentile, with a price-to-salary ratio of $195/sqft to $36.37/hr, resulting in a ratio of 5.36. Although the city's cost of living is not extremely low, its relative affordability compared to peer cities can be a talent attraction advantage, as workers may not require significant wage premiums to maintain their standard of living. However, the 1.6% year-over-year increase in PSF suggests that affordability may be decreasing over time.

Labor Force Growth

The civilian labor force in Kansas City is shrinking at a rate of -2.09% year-over-year, ranking at the 20th percentile. This contraction in the labor force supply poses a structural headwind for hiring, as businesses may struggle to find qualified workers to fill open positions. The declining labor force can limit the city's ability to support rapid business growth or expansion.

Building Permits

The number of residential building permits in Kansas City has decreased by 3.34% year-over-year, ranking at the 41st percentile. This decline in permits suggests that the city's housing supply is not expanding at a rate that keeps pace with demand, which can lead to future affordability challenges and make it more difficult for workers to relocate to the area.

Days on Market

The median days on market for homes in Kansas City is 50 days, with a year-over-year decrease of 2.0%. This relatively fast pace of home sales, ranking at the 18th percentile, indicates a competitive market that may be challenging for relocating workers to find affordable housing. The quick turnover of homes can make it difficult for businesses to attract talent from other areas, as workers may be deterred by the competitive housing market.

Office Economy

Kansas City's professional and office worker share is relatively low, ranking at the 26th percentile, with a composite score of 1.90. This suggests that the city's talent pool is not as deep in specialized office roles, making it less suited for businesses in the tech, finance, or consulting sectors. However, the city may be more attractive to businesses in industries that are less dependent on a highly specialized office workforce.

In conclusion, Kansas City offers businesses a strong labor market with rising wages and a relatively tight unemployment rate, making it an attractive location for companies that can adapt to these conditions. However, the single biggest risk or constraint for businesses considering Kansas City is the shrinking labor force, which can limit the city's ability to support rapid growth or expansion, and may require companies to invest in talent development and retention strategies to overcome this challenge.
