Economic Analysis

The Baltimore-Columbia-Towson metro area has an overall grade of B- with a composite score ranking it at the 46.2th percentile out of 50 US metros. This city's economic character is most defined by its weak labor demand, with a labor demand composite score in the bottom tier at the 14th percentile, and its relatively high cost of living, which ranks in the 80th percentile for affordability. The combination of a -0.37% employment growth rate and a -0.648% weekly hours deviation from trend suggests a contracting labor market.

Labor Demand

The employment growth rate in Baltimore-Columbia-Towson is -0.37% year-over-year, and weekly hours are deviating -0.648% from the city's own 12-month baseline. This combination signals a contraction in labor demand, indicating that the city is experiencing a decline in job creation and hours worked. As a result, businesses may face a survivor squeeze, where remaining workers absorb the load of eliminated roles.

Unemployment

The unemployment rate in Baltimore-Columbia-Towson is 3.90%, which places it near the median at the 50th percentile. This rate suggests that the labor market has some slack, making it slightly easier for businesses to hire compared to tighter markets. However, the relatively low unemployment rate also means that businesses may still face some competition for talent.

Wage Growth

The year-over-year wage growth in Baltimore-Columbia-Towson is +3.93%, which is near the median at the 54th percentile. This moderate wage growth rate suggests that labor costs for employers are rising, but at a manageable pace. Workers in the area will also experience an increase in purchasing power, which can benefit local consumer demand.

Cost of Living

Baltimore-Columbia-Towson has a cost of living score that ranks in the 80th percentile for affordability, with a PSF of $219/sqft and average hourly earnings of $36.97/hr, resulting in a ratio of 5.92. The fact that PSF is falling by -1.4% year-over-year contributes to the city's high affordability score. This makes the city more attractive for talent attraction, as workers can afford a higher quality of life without requiring significant wage premiums.

Labor Force Growth

The civilian labor force in Baltimore-Columbia-Towson is growing at a rate of -0.22% year-over-year, which is near the median at the 56th percentile. This slight contraction in labor force supply may pose a structural headwind for hiring, as the pool of available workers is shrinking. Businesses may need to adapt their recruitment strategies to attract talent from a smaller labor pool.

Building Permits

The number of residential building permits in Baltimore-Columbia-Towson is decreasing by -2.81% year-over-year, which is near the median at the 44th percentile. This decline in building permits suggests that the housing supply is tightening, which may lead to decreased affordability and increased competition for housing in the future. This could negatively impact the city's ability to attract and accommodate a growing workforce.

Days on Market

The current median days on market in Baltimore-Columbia-Towson is 36 days, with a year-over-year increase of +9.1%. This places the city in the top tier at the 88th percentile for days on market, indicating a slower market where homes sit longer before being sold. For workers relocating to the city, this means a more accessible and less competitive housing market, allowing them to find and purchase homes with relative ease.

Office Economy

The share of jobs in professional and office sectors in Baltimore-Columbia-Towson is below average, ranking in the 22nd percentile. This suggests that the city has a relatively shallow talent pool for tech, finance, consulting, and HQ decisions, making it less suited for businesses that rely heavily on these sectors. However, the city may be more suitable for industries with fewer specialized roles, such as industrial or logistics-dominant economies.

The Baltimore-Columbia-Towson metro area offers businesses a relatively affordable cost of living and a moderate pace of wage growth, but its weak labor demand and tightening housing supply pose significant risks. The single biggest constraint for businesses considering this location is the city's limited labor market growth and potential survivor squeeze, which may impact their ability to attract and retain talent in the long term.
