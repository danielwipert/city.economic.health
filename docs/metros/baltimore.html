Economic Analysis

The Baltimore-Columbia-Towson metro area has an overall grade of B, ranking in the 53.7th percentile among 50 US metros, with a composite score driven largely by its above-average unemployment rate of 4.00% and strong wage growth of +4.94% year-over-year. The city's economic character is also defined by its cost of living, with a PSF to wages ratio of $216/sqft to $37.54/hr, indicating a relatively affordable market. This affordability, combined with low unemployment, presents a unique economic landscape.

Labor Demand

The employment growth rate in Baltimore-Columbia-Towson is -0.10% year-over-year, accompanied by a -0.673% deviation in weekly hours from its own trend, signaling a contraction in labor demand. This combination indicates a lack of genuine demand expansion, suggesting that the city's job market is not experiencing significant growth. The negative hours deviation further supports this conclusion, pointing to a potential survivor squeeze where remaining workers may absorb the workload of eliminated roles.

Unemployment

The unemployment rate in Baltimore-Columbia-Towson is 4.00%, ranking in the 60th percentile, which indicates a relatively tight labor market with some slack. For a business trying to hire in this market, the low unemployment rate implies that it may be moderately challenging to find suitable candidates, potentially leading to upward pressure on wages. However, the presence of some slack suggests that hiring is still feasible without extreme difficulty.

Wage Growth

The year-over-year wage growth in Baltimore-Columbia-Towson is +4.94%, placing it above average in the 68th percentile. This rate of wage growth is relatively fast, suggesting that labor costs for employers are rising. On the other hand, workers in the area are experiencing an increase in purchasing power, which can be beneficial for local consumer demand.

Cost of Living

Baltimore-Columbia-Towson has a cost of living score in the 86th percentile, with a PSF to wages ratio of $216/sqft to $37.54/hr, and the PSF is falling by -2.3% year-over-year. This indicates that the city is relatively affordable compared to its peers, making it attractive for talent without necessitating significant wage premiums. The falling PSF relative to wages is a key driver of this affordability, enhancing the city's appeal for both workers and businesses.

Labor Force Growth

The civilian labor force in Baltimore-Columbia-Towson is growing at a rate of +1.23% year-over-year, ranking in the 74th percentile. This positive growth rate signifies that the workforce supply is expanding, which is a favorable condition for businesses looking to hire, as it implies a larger and potentially more diverse talent pool.

Building Permits

The year-over-year change in residential building permits in Baltimore-Columbia-Towson is -10.35%, indicating a tightening in housing supply. This decline suggests that future affordability and workforce accommodation may be at risk, as a lack of new housing developments can lead to increased competition for existing homes and potentially higher prices.

Days on Market

The current median days on market for homes in Baltimore-Columbia-Towson is 39 days, with a year-over-year increase of +5.4%, placing it in the top tier of the 86th percentile. This slower market means that homes are sitting longer before being sold, which can be beneficial for workers relocating to the city, as it indicates a less competitive and more accessible housing market.

Office Economy

Baltimore-Columbia-Towson has an office economy score in the 28th percentile, indicating a relatively shallow professional talent pool. This suggests that the city is less suited for businesses that require a deep knowledge-economy talent pool, such as tech, finance, or consulting firms, but may be more appropriate for industries with different workforce requirements.

The Baltimore-Columbia-Towson metro area offers businesses a unique combination of affordability, low unemployment, and strong wage growth, making it an attractive location for certain types of industries. However, the single biggest risk or constraint for decision-makers is the tightening housing supply, signaled by the decline in building permits, which could impact future workforce accommodation and affordability.
