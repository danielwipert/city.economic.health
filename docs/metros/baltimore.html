Economic Analysis

Baltimore-Columbia-Towson earns an overall grade of A-, with a score of 64.7 out of 100, indicating it is better than most of the 50 metros. What most defines this city's economy right now is its strong wage growth, with pay rising 6.4% over the past year, and a low unemployment rate of 3.5%, which is better than 84% of the metros.

Labor Demand

Whether employers are adding jobs, based on job growth over the past year and changes in weekly work hours.

The city is losing jobs, with employment growth at -0.2% over the past year, and workers' hours are only slightly rising, by 0.1%. This means the job market is not particularly strong, with a score of 24, indicating it is below average compared to other metros.

Unemployment

The share of people who want a job but can't find one; lower is better.

The unemployment rate of 3.5% makes it hard for employers to hire, as it is better than 84% of the metros, indicating a tight labor market. This low unemployment rate means employers may face wage pressure and difficulty finding qualified workers.

Wage Growth

How much average hourly pay rose over the past year.

Pay rose 6.4% over the past year, which is a top-tier increase, with a score of 90, indicating it is better than 90% of the metros. This strong wage growth means employers face rising labor costs, but workers benefit from increased purchasing power.

Cost of Living

How many hours a local worker needs to work to pay for one square foot of a home, and whether that's improving.

Housing is relatively affordable, with a price per square foot of $213 and hourly pay of $37.72, and it is getting even more affordable as prices are falling 2.7% over the past year, earning a top-tier score of 90. This affordability is a talent attraction advantage, as workers can afford to live and work in the city.

Labor Force Growth

Whether the number of people working or looking for work is growing.

The pool of workers is growing slowly, at 0.4% over the past year, which is above average, with a score of 64. This moderate growth means hiring may be somewhat easier, but the city is not experiencing a surge in new workers.

Building Permits

How many new homes builders got approval for, compared with a year ago.

New home approvals fell 2.8% over the past year, which is near the median, with a score of 52. This decline in building permits may signal a future housing supply squeeze, which could impact affordability and workforce attraction.

Days on Market

How long a typical home for sale takes to find a buyer; about 35 to 80 days is healthiest.

A typical home takes 46 days to sell, and this is getting slower, with a 12.2% increase over the past year, earning a top-tier score of 92. This indicates a healthy housing market, where buyers have time to find a home and sellers can sell at a reasonable pace.

Office Economy

The share of jobs in office-based fields like tech, finance and professional services.

The city's office-based workforce is near the median, with a score of 48, indicating it is not particularly dominant in tech, finance, or professional services. This means the city may not be the best fit for businesses that require a deep knowledge-economy talent pool.

The bottom line is that Baltimore-Columbia-Towson's biggest strength is its strong wage growth and affordable cost of living, making it an attractive place for workers. However, its biggest risk is its slow labor demand and declining building permits, which may impact its ability to attract and retain businesses and workers in the long term.
